Pair-Conditioned Verification of Literature-Mined Biotic Interactions}

\begin{abstract}
Literature-mining pipelines that populate biotic-interaction databases propose candidate
(taxon, relation, taxon) triples by co-occurrence, then decide which to keep with a
\emph{sentence-level} classifier: does this passage describe an interaction? A candidate is retrieved
precisely because its passage names two taxa near an interaction term, so that question is answered
before it is asked; what needs deciding is whether the passage asserts an interaction between
\emph{these two}. We test the claim with a controlled comparison: one encoder, one 48k-row
teacher-labelled corpus, three seeds, differing only in what the model is shown. On 437
expert-graded candidates, telling the model which pair the candidate is about raises AUPRC from
$0.851$ to $0.919$ and F1 from $0.775$ to $0.871$ (75 items fixed against 22 broken, McNemar
$p = 1.3\times10^{-7}$), at every one of 99 thresholds; adding the relation term to the query buys
nothing further. The 32B model that labelled the corpus, asked the same questions zero-shot, shows
the same gap at twice the size ($0.789 \to 0.943$). The same comparison on the public BioRED corpus, with candidates built the same
way, gives $0.738 \to 0.843$ on the BioCreative VIII test set ($p = 5.9\times10^{-148}$) and
$+9.6$ points of its standard entity-pair F1, and the gain lies where the argument puts it: none on
sentences that name two entities, $+0.120$ on sentences that name more. We analyse the
remaining errors, test candidate rules, and report the deployed model: a 110M-parameter
pair-conditioned verifier that also labels direction in four categories, at 33 candidates per
second on a CPU.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Biotic-interaction databases such as GloBI \citep{poelen2014global} are populated in part by text
mining. A retrieval system proposes candidate triples $(s_1, r, s_2)$ together with the passage that
allegedly supports them, and something must decide which candidates are worth a curator's attention.
The natural choice, and the common one, is a sentence-level interaction classifier: show it the
passage and ask whether it describes a biotic interaction.

That question is answered before it is asked. Candidates are proposed by co-occurrence: a passage
naming two taxa near an interaction term yields a candidate whether or not it asserts that
interaction between those two. Every candidate therefore arrives already satisfying, lexically, the
property a sentence-level classifier was trained to detect --- in the pipeline we study, on all
$175{,}588$ candidates of its retrieval pool (\S\ref{sec:setting}). What actually needs deciding is
narrower: whether the passage asserts an interaction between \emph{these two} taxa. When a passage
names three organisms it can support one candidate and refute the other two, and a classifier that
sees only the passage must give all three the same answer.

We make this argument testable rather than rhetorical. We train the same 110M-parameter encoder on
the same teacher-labelled corpus three times --- reading the passage alone, the passage with the
candidate pair, and the passage with the whole candidate triple --- and compare them on a 437-item
benchmark graded by a domain expert (\S\ref{sec:results}). Everything except the input is held
fixed, so differences between them are attributable to the formulation and to nothing else. Telling
the model which pair it is judging wins by a wide and significant margin; telling it the relation
term as well does not add to it. We then rebuild the setting on BioRED \citep{luo2022biored}, a public
corpus with gold entity and relation annotations, and the result replicates there with the gain in
the predicted place: absent on sentences that name only two entities, large on sentences that name
more.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt\parskip0pt\topsep2pt
\item A controlled comparison of sentence-level, pair-conditioned and triple-conditioned
      verification on one encoder and one corpus, replicated on a public benchmark and in a
      zero-shot 32B model, with the gain located where the argument says it should be
      (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\item An account of what the pair query buys --- disambiguation between the pairs a passage names,
      and a check on candidates whose arguments are not organisms --- and a bound showing that
      checking a triple's components separately cannot reach the largest error class
      (\S\ref{sec:results}, \S\ref{sec:decomp}).
\item Deterministic candidate rules, validated on 42k training rows before evaluation, measured as
      a small precision gain rather than a large one (\S\ref{sec:rules}).
\item A deployed configuration with a fixed threshold, four-way direction output and CPU
      throughput, and five negative results (\S\ref{sec:deploy}, Appendix~\ref{sec:negative}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Where candidates come from.}
BiotXplorer \citep{ruch2024biotxplorer} proposes candidate biotic-interaction triples from the
literature indexed by SIB Literature Services \citep{gobeill2020sibils}. For each passage it matches
taxon surface forms against a taxonomy and interaction surface forms against a vocabulary derived
from the Relation Ontology, and emits every resulting $(s_1, r, s_2)$ combination with the passage,
the matched surface strings, and their canonical forms. This is the application we build for, and
we use it only as the source of candidates: no component of it is a baseline in this paper.

The problem this paper addresses was diagnosed by BiotXplorer's own authors.
\citet{ruch2024biotxplorer} graded 100 random BiotXplorer triples, found that the system identified
the interacting species with a precision of 31\%, and named the main cause of error: ``instances
where passages listed multiple species, which can be automatically filtered out.'' We take the
diagnosis as given and question only the remedy. On our benchmark, passages naming three or more
taxa hold 154 of the 246 interacting pairs (63\%), so filtering them out would discard most of what
the pipeline exists to find. The alternative we test is to keep those passages and decide each
candidate in them separately.

\paragraph{Why the sentence-level question is constant here.}
We pulled the retrieval pool in full: $175{,}588$ candidate rows over $165{,}325$ distinct passages
and $115{,}404$ distinct triplet keys. Both taxon surface forms and the interaction surface form are
non-empty on all $175{,}588$ rows, because the presence of an interaction term is the pool's
construction filter, and an independent lexical scan for GloBI interaction vocabulary fires on
$99.82\%$ of the same passages. A classifier trained to separate passages with interaction language
from passages without it is, on this distribution, being asked about a constant. The missing input
is not an annotator artefact but the query the pipeline itself issued.

\paragraph{Benchmark.}
One benchmark of 437 rows, 246 positive, in three blocks of different provenance; we report every
block rather than pooling them into a number that hides their disagreement.
\textsc{Biotx100} is retrieved candidates, each the top-ranked candidate for its passage, graded by
a domain expert on four axes: whether each taxon surface form was correctly resolved, whether the
relation was correct, and whether the whole triple is supported by its passage. Half were drawn
from pairs already recorded in GloBI and half at random, and after removing three training
duplicates it contributes 97 rows: 50 GloBI-matched with 43 positive, 47 random with 32 positive.
It is therefore enriched for known interactors and for top-ranked candidates, which is why its
positive rate is far above the 31\% \citet{ruch2024biotxplorer} measured on random triples at any
rank. \textsc{Reject50} is candidates the
retrieval pipeline discarded, curated for the ones it should have kept: 41 clean rows, 9 positive.
It is the paper's recall-side instrument. \textsc{Test299} is 299 pair-level items from expert
curation, $0.542$ positive, and is where passages most often name several taxa.

The blocks do not carry the same gold. \textsc{Biotx100} is the only block graded at the level of
the whole triple; \textsc{Test299} was curated pair-level and its stored relation term was attached
by retrieval rather than by an annotator. We therefore evaluate everywhere at the level all three
share --- \emph{do these two taxa interact in this passage} --- and use the triple-level grades only to
diagnose (\S\ref{sec:decomp}). This understates what triple conditioning buys, since a candidate
with the right pair and the wrong relation counts as correct, and we prefer understating it to
scoring two blocks against a relation term nobody checked.

\paragraph{Query representation.}
Each candidate carries a canonical string and the surface form matched in the passage. On
\textsc{Biotx100} these differ for $s_1$, $s_2$ and $r$ on 34, 38 and 59 of 100 rows, and the
canonical string is absent from the passage on 31, 27 and 54. We always query with the surface form,
which is what the model can see.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.}
Thresholds are where this kind of evaluation goes wrong most easily, so the policy is fixed in
advance. A model's reported operating point is either \textbf{pre-specified} --- chosen on its own
training-development split, never on the benchmark --- or \textbf{block-held-out}: fitted on two of
the three source blocks and applied to the third, so that every row is scored by a threshold that
never saw it and every row is used. Comparisons between models use the same policy for both.
Intervals are $20{,}000$-sample bootstraps or Clopper--Pearson; McNemar tests
\citep{mcnemar1947note} carry a continuity correction. Where three seeds exist we report the per-seed mean and standard deviation, and where we
report the average of three checkpoints' probabilities we call it an ensemble. The benchmark ships
an \texttt{in\_train} flag on 9 rows; a 5-gram Jaccard scan against all 48{,}338 training passages
finds 3 more at Jaccard $1.000$, all in \textsc{Biotx100}. All 12 are excluded throughout.

The gold is pair-level and therefore order-free: \emph{do these two taxa interact}, in either
direction. A model that reads the candidate in order is scored the same way, as the higher of its
two scores over the two argument orders --- the probability that the pair interacts in at least one
direction. We fix this before scoring because the alternative penalises a model for rejecting a
correctly paired candidate whose stated relation runs backwards, which the gold does not count as an
error. The sentence-level model reads no candidate and is order-free already.

Two things about this benchmark's history should be on the record. It is not untouched: during the
development of the verifier it was scored for roughly a hundred model configurations, so the
comparisons in this paper are between recipes chosen with it in view, and the controlled comparison
of \S\ref{sec:results} is the result we would expect to survive that. And one gold label has been
changed since the benchmark was frozen: a \textsc{Test299} candidate pairing \emph{Acanthamoeba}
with \emph{Pseudomonas}, revised from positive to negative after the curator, annotating direction
without model scores, recorded that the two co-occur in a shared host rather than interacting.

\section{Two formulations, one corpus}
\label{sec:verifier}

\paragraph{The teacher.}
Labels come from a locally hosted 32B open-weight instruction model \citep{qwen2025technical},
prompted zero-shot at temperature 0 and asked, per candidate, whether the passage supports
\emph{this} triple. Its labels therefore vary across candidates that share a passage. Against 150
human-graded items its mislabel rate is $22/150 = 14.7\%$, Clopper--Pearson $[9.4\%, 21.4\%]$, split
10 false positives and 12 false negatives.

\paragraph{The corpus.}
48{,}338 teacher-labelled rows, $0.343$ positive, drawn from the same retrieval pool as the
benchmark (disjoint passages): a 20{,}000-row base sample, 16{,}546 \emph{pair-contrast} rows,
6{,}201 actively sampled rows, and 5{,}591 rows with the candidate's argument order reversed. The
pair-contrast block is what gives the corpus its point: 2{,}017 passages enter as several rows that
are identical in the passage and differ only in the candidate and the label.

\paragraph{Three models, one recipe.}
All three are cross-encoders on BiomedBERT-base \citep{gu2021domain}
(\texttt{microsoft/BiomedNLP-BiomedBERT-base-uncased-abstract-fulltext}, 110M parameters), distilled
from the teacher's labels \citep{bucilua2006model,hinton2015distilling} with the same recipe:
AdamW at learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$ and weight decay $0.01$, a linear schedule with 10\% warm-up,
batch size 32, inputs of at most 256 wordpieces, three epochs, gradient clipping at 1.0, and the
checkpoint with the best development AUPRC, on a 10\% development split grouped by taxon pair so
that no pair spans training and development. Three seeds each; one run takes about four minutes on a
single A100. They differ only in their input.
The \textbf{sentence-level baseline} reads the passage alone. It is the formulation a pipeline
reaches for first, and on rows that share a passage it is shown the same input with different
labels, which it cannot fit. The \textbf{pair-conditioned} model reads the two candidate taxa,
$s_1$ \texttt{[SEP]} $s_2$, as segment~A and the passage as segment~B
\citep{nogueira2019passage}. The \textbf{triple-conditioned verifier} reads the whole candidate,
$s_1$ \texttt{[SEP]} $r$ \texttt{[SEP]} $s_2$. The middle model separates what the pair buys from
what the relation adds.

\subsection{Conditioning on the pair}
\label{sec:res-main}
Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the comparison. Telling the model which pair a candidate is about raises
per-seed AUPRC from $0.851 \pm 0.006$ to $0.919 \pm 0.005$ and, at block-held-out thresholds, F1
from $0.775$ to $0.871$, precision and recall both. On decision correctness the pair-conditioned
model fixes 75 items and breaks 22, McNemar $p = 1.3\times10^{-7}$. The advantage does not depend on
where we cut: it is ahead at every one of 99 thresholds, by at least $0.058$ F1
(Figure~\ref{fig:threshold}).

Adding the relation term to the query does not add to this. The triple-conditioned model is also far
ahead of the sentence-level one ($p = 6.8\times10^{-5}$), but against the pair-conditioned model it
fixes 20 items and breaks 31 ($p = 0.16$), and its AUPRC is, if anything, lower. What the decision
needs is the pair. The one place the relation term earns its keep is at strict operating points:
at $\tau = 0.5$ both reach precision $0.913$, the triple model at recall $0.768$ and the pair model
at $0.683$ (Table~\ref{tab:curve}).

\paragraph{The same gap in the teacher.}
The formulation effect is not a property of small encoders. Asked the sentence-level question
zero-shot, the 32B teacher scores AUPRC $0.789$, below the 110M sentence-level student; asked about
the pair, $0.943$ (Table~\ref{tab:main}). Its gap, $+0.154$, is twice the student's, and it sits in
the same place: $+0.180$ on passages naming three or more taxa against $+0.104$ on the rest, where
the student's is $+0.084$ against $+0.042$. A model three hundred times smaller that is asked the
right question beats the teacher asked the wrong one by $0.13$ AUPRC. As with the student, the
relation term adds nothing ($0.946$ against $0.943$). Against its own teacher the pair-conditioned
student gives up $0.027$ AUPRC and nothing in F1 at block-held-out thresholds ($0.871$ against
$0.870$), at 33 candidates per second on a CPU.

Table~\ref{tab:where} locates the gain, and it is the most direct test of the argument. On passages
that name three or more taxa, the pair query is worth $+0.084$ AUPRC. On passages naming two or
fewer it is worth less, $+0.042$, and the split below shows why it is worth anything there at all:
the candidates come from automatic matching, and in 41\% of them one ``taxon'' is a string TaxoNERD
does not recognise as an organism in the passage --- an organ, an author's name, a vernacular term.
Where both arguments are recognised taxa and the passage names no third one, the case in which the
sentence-level question and the pair question coincide, the pair query adds $+0.026$ on 33 items;
where an argument is not a taxon it adds $+0.052$, and $+0.180$ when the passage also names others.
The pair query does two things, then: it tells the model which of several pairs to judge, and it
lets the model check that what it is judging is a pair of organisms. BioRED, whose entities are
gold-annotated, isolates the first.

The biodiversity benchmark is ours, singly annotated, and modest in size. To test the claim where
anyone can check it, we rebuild the same setting on BioRED \citep{luo2022biored}, a public corpus
of PubMed abstracts with expert-annotated entities and relations. Candidates are generated exactly
as the biodiversity pipeline generates them, by co-occurrence: every pair of distinct annotated
concepts co-mentioned in one sentence, restricted to the eight entity-type pairs that carry
relations in training, with the sentence as passage. A candidate is positive when BioRED relates
the two concepts anywhere in the abstract, a document-level label whose noise every model shares.
We follow the protocol of \citet{lai2025bioredirect}: models train on the 500 abstracts of BioRED's
training and development splits (15{,}640 candidates), select their epoch and threshold on its 100
test abstracts, and are scored on the 400 abstracts of the BioCreative VIII test set
\citep{islamaj2024overview}. BioRED has no retrieved relation term, so only the sentence-level
and pair-conditioned formulations are compared, trained with the recipe of \S\ref{sec:verifier}.

The result (Table~\ref{tab:biored}) reproduces the biodiversity one in both its parts.
Conditioning on the pair raises AUPRC from $0.738$ to $0.843$ and F1 from $0.718$ to $0.817$. And
the gain sits in the same place: on candidates from sentences that name only two concepts the two
models are indistinguishable ($0.909$ against $0.902$; per seed $0.899$--$0.919$ against
$0.892$--$0.914$), while on those from sentences naming more, which are 97\% of the candidates,
conditioning is worth $+0.120$. Marking the two mentions in the passage, the standard relation
extraction input \citep{zhou2022improved}, adds nothing to the pair query ($p = 0.08$). The
teacher, asked the sentence-level question, accepts 92\% of its sample (AUPRC $0.566$ at a base
rate of $0.48$); asked about the pair, $0.813$, still behind the trained pair-conditioned model on
these document-level labels ($0.860$ on the same sample).

On BioRED's standard document-level metric the pair-conditioned verifier reaches an entity-pair F1
of $67.1$, against $57.5$ for the sentence-level one. Systems that read the whole abstract reach
$74.1$ to $75.3$ (PubMedBERT, BioREx and BioREDirect; \citealp{lai2023biorex,lai2025bioredirect})
and zero-shot GPT-4 $42.9$ \citep{islamaj2024overview}. Much of that gap is structural: a quarter of
the related pairs are never co-mentioned in one sentence, which caps any sentence-scope verifier at
recall $0.749$. Given the whole abstract instead, with every mention of the two concepts marked, the
same model and recipe reach $67.0$: recall rises to $0.697$, against a ceiling of $0.912$, and
precision falls to $0.646$. What the published systems add is document-level modelling our passage
recipe does not attempt --- relation-type supervision, typed markers, tuned schedules --- which is
not the question this paper asks.

Degrading the trained triple model's query at inference, with weights and thresholds unchanged,
tells the same story from the other side (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). Deleting both taxa collapses
ranking from $0.909$ to $0.677$ against a base rate of $0.563$, and emptying the query entirely gives
$0.641$. Replacing or shuffling the relation, pair kept, barely moves ranking ($0.888$, $0.904$): the
model leans on the pair, which is what the trained pair-only model already showed.

The empty-query arm is \emph{not} the sentence-level baseline: it is a model trained with the query
and deprived of it, and it does far worse ($0.641$) than a model trained without the query from the
start ($0.851$). Ablating an input at inference measures dependence on it; it does not stand in for
training without it, which is why the comparison of Table~\ref{tab:main} trains all three.

Nor does the gain come from the corpus's pair-contrast design. A triple-conditioned model trained on
the 20{,}000-row base sample alone reaches per-seed AUPRC $0.918 \pm 0.003$, against $0.909 \pm 0.004$
for the full corpus, and the same block-held-out F1 as the pair model, $0.871$. The input
formulation is what buys the improvement; the extra supervision buys nothing we can measure here.

\subsection{Recall side}
\label{sec:recall}
\textsc{Reject50} asks whether the candidates the retrieval pipeline discarded contain recoverable
positives, and it is too small to rank the models: nine positives, so one item moves recall by
eleven points. At block-held-out thresholds the sentence-level model recovers 7 of the 9 by
re-admitting 15 of the 32 correct rejections (F1 $0.452$), the pair-conditioned model recovers 4
while re-admitting 7 ($0.400$), and the triple-conditioned model recovers 6 while re-admitting 5
($0.600$). We read only the qualitative point: the discarded candidates do contain recoverable
interactions, and a model that reads the candidate finds them at a lower cost in wrong
re-admissions than one that reads only the passage.

\section{What the remaining errors are made of}
\label{sec:errors}
\label{sec:decomp}

The pair-conditioned model's 39 false positives at its block-held-out operating point are not
homogeneous: 27 come from passages naming three or more taxa, and 17 have an argument TaxoNERD does
not recognise as a taxon. Read by hand, they sort into a small number of mechanisms
(examples in Table~\ref{tab:examples}, Appendix~\ref{app:examples}; the per-row table is released): \emph{pair-binding},
where both taxa and the relation are correct but the relation's argument is a third organism;
\emph{co-listing}, where the two taxa are members of one enumeration and interact with neither each
other nor anything in particular; \emph{negation}, where the passage asserts the opposite;
\emph{same organism}, where the two ``taxa'' are one organism named twice or an organism and its own
clade; candidates whose relation term names no biotic interaction at all (\emph{relative to},
\emph{hybridization}, \emph{migration}); and \emph{entity} failures, where a taxon string is a
taxonomic author, a place, an organ, or an adjective inside a pathogen's name (``\emph{equine}
influenza virus'', which the pair-conditioned model already rejects). A further handful read as gold errors and are discussed in the Limitations.

\paragraph{A bound on component-wise verification.}
The pair-binding class has a structural property worth stating, because it rules out the obvious
alternative architecture. Its members have both taxa correctly resolved \emph{and} the correct
relation; what is wrong is a binding that exists only in retrieval's combinatorics. A verifier that
checks $s_1$, $r$ and $s_2$ separately --- however good each check is --- cannot see it. On the 97
clean \textsc{Biotx100} candidates, where triple-level grades make the classes countable, 15 of the
32 unsupported candidates are pair-binding failures (the rest: 9 entity, 8 predicate), a share of
$0.47$, Clopper--Pearson $[0.29, 0.65]$. A component-wise verifier accepting a fraction $r$ of the 65
supported triples therefore also accepts all 15, so its precision is at most $65r/(65r+15)$:
$0.81$ at full recall, $[0.76, 0.88]$ across that interval. Only a decision that reads the pair
\emph{in context} can go above it.

\section{Candidate rules}
\label{sec:rules}

Several of these mechanisms are questions about the candidate rather than the passage --- whether
two strings name one organism, whether a word is a surname, whether ``relative to'' is an
interaction --- and a classifier is a poor instrument for them. We answer them with eight
deterministic rejection rules applied before the verifier decides (Appendix~\ref{app:rules}). No
rule has a parameter fitted to evaluation labels, and since four were written after reading the
false positives above, each had to pass a criterion fixed in advance on the 42{,}747 training rows
(at least 85\% of what it rejects is teacher-negative); they were then frozen, recorded by hash and
evaluated once. The result (Table~\ref{tab:rules}) is smaller than the motivation suggested, and the
reason is the headline of this paper. On the pair-conditioned model the rules buy $0.009$ to $0.018$
precision for $0.008$ to $0.020$ recall, F1 unchanged ($p = 1.00$): of its 39 false positives they
catch 6, because a model told which pair it is judging already rejects most of what they were
written to reject. On the triple-conditioned model, which leans on its relation term, they do more
(precision $0.820 \to 0.862$, 14 fixed and 5 broken, $p = 0.067$). Both are upper estimates, since
half the rules were chosen with this benchmark's errors in view; their case in deployment is
transparency, since each rejection names the rule that made it. A taxonomy gate is no substitute:
surnames, clades and transposon families all resolve in a taxonomy (Appendix~\ref{sec:notfixed}).

In the pipeline, verification runs as one 110M-parameter model with two heads. It differs from the
pair-conditioned model of \S\ref{sec:verifier} in three respects, each of which we adopted during
development: the two taxa are given in alphabetical order, so the input is identical whichever way
round retrieval presents the pair; they are marked in place in the passage (\texttt{@}\ldots\texttt{@},
\texttt{\#}\ldots\texttt{\#}) rather than only named in segment~A; and the training labels are the
teacher's pair-level judgments. Its interaction threshold, $0.5$, was set before evaluation and never
tuned. On the 437 rows it reaches AUPRC $0.934$, and precision $0.851$, recall $0.955$, F1 $0.900$;
with the candidate rules, precision $0.869$, recall $0.947$, F1 $0.907$ (6 items fixed, 2 broken,
$p = 0.29$).\footnote{The \emph{co\_listed} rule is omitted from this configuration: on the deployed
model's accept set it removes one false positive at the cost of three true ones.} It needs no GPU: on
eight CPU threads it scores $32.7$ candidates per second with both heads, after a one-off $7.5$\,s to
load, which is faster than the pipeline retrieves them, so every candidate is verified rather than a
sample.

The second head reports the interaction's \emph{direction} in four categories. \textsc{Forward} and
\textsc{Reverse} say which taxon acts on the other; they come from a direction score that is exactly
antisymmetric in the two marked taxa, and are reported only when it is confident, otherwise the
answer is \textsc{Uncertain}. \textsc{Bidirectional} is assigned when the relation is mutual by
definition --- mutualism, symbiosis, competition, co-infection, \emph{interacts with} --- as listed
in a relation-polarity lexicon derived from the Relation Ontology; for those, neither taxon is the
subject and the model is not asked. Expert curation of the benchmark scores reversed triples as
correct (Appendix~\ref{sec:notfixed}), so direction is evaluated on a separate expert annotation of 97
candidates (Table~\ref{tab:direction}): the model answers 74\% of the 84 with a direction and is right
on $0.887$ of those, leaves 9 of the 12 the expert could not decide as \textsc{Uncertain}, and labels
the one bidirectional item \textsc{Bidirectional}.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Classifiers that answer an easier question.}
That a classifier can score well by exploiting how its data were assembled is among the
best-established results in NLP. Partial-input baselines recover much of the label from the
hypothesis alone in natural language inference
\citep{gururangan-etal-2018-annotation,poliak-etal-2018-hypothesis} and from the passage alone in
reading comprehension \citep{kaushik-lipton-2018-much}, and such rules hold in distribution while
failing under deployment \citep{geirhos-etal-2020-shortcut,du-etal-2024-shortcut}; the closest
task-level precedent is fact verification \citep{thorne2018fever}, where a claim-only classifier
matches evidence-aware models because of how the corpus was built \citep{schuster-etal-2019-towards}. Our sentence-level
baseline is a partial-input model in exactly this sense, and the specific feature here is that the
missing input is the query the pipeline itself issued rather than an annotator artefact
\citep{wiegand-etal-2019-detection,parmar-etal-2023-dont}. \citet{feng-etal-2019-misleading}
caution that partial-input results license claims about models and corpora rather than about
tasks, and we keep ours to the former.

\paragraph{The argument-ignoring failure is already named.}
Distant supervision assumes that a sentence containing two related entities expresses the relation
\citep{mintz2009distant}, and its standard relaxation exists because it does not
\citep{riedel2010modeling}. Since TACRED an instance has been the triple (sentence, $e_1$, $e_2$)
rather than the sentence \citep{zhang2017position}, and how the pair is injected into the encoder is
worth several F1 points \citep{soares2019matching,wu2019enriching,zhou2022improved}.
\citet{rosenman2020exposing} name our failure exactly, as an ``event heuristic'' that classifies on
(sentence, relation) while ignoring the arguments. \emph{We claim no part of that diagnosis.} What is
ours is a controlled measurement of what it costs in a verification setting built by co-occurrence
retrieval, located by block; a bound on component-wise remedies; and the candidate rules. The
entity-type shortcut usually invoked for such errors \citep{peng2020learning,mtumbuka2024entity} is
unavailable here, since both slots are filled from one inventory of taxon surface forms. Error
attribution across pipeline stages is standard and easy to get wrong
\citep{taille-etal-2020-lets,ai2023endtoend}.

\paragraph{Conditioning on the query.}
Casting a relation as a question about a named entity \citep{levy2017zero}, or as an entailment
hypothesis verbalising the relation between two of them \citep{obamuyide2018zero,sainz2021label},
is the reformulation our input format instantiates, and the resulting decision --- does this passage
support this specific claim --- has the shape of scientific claim verification
\citep{wadden2020fact}, and of validating a knowledge-graph triple against text
\citep{lehmann2012defacto}, with the difference that those systems retrieve evidence for a given
triple while here the passage is fixed by the retrieval that proposed the triple and the question is
whether that passage, and no other, supports it. Distilling a large model's judgments into a small student is established
practice \citep{west2022symbolic,gekhman2023trueteacher}. Selective classification thresholds a
score instead of issuing a verdict \citep{geifman2017selective}, which is how extraction is made
useful to human abstractors: \citet{swaminathan2024selective} abstain under calibrated thresholds to
automate $57$--$95\%$ of clinical chart abstraction at controlled error.

\paragraph{Text mining for interaction databases.}
GloBI \citep{poelen2014global} is populated in part by text mining \citep{dimitrova2020semantic}.
Judging an extracted fact before it reaches a curator is an old idea
\citep{rodriguezesteban2006imitating}, as is evaluating such tools by whether they help the curator
\citep{hirschman2012biocuration,britan2018nexta5,lee2018scaling}. \citet{cuzick2023interspecies},
independently, take the interacting pair rather than the passage as the annotation unit --- the unit
our pair-binding class isolates. Large language models are applied to interaction extraction at
scale \citep{keck2025extracting,farrell2024landscape,dsouza2025mining,zou2026llm}, but as
extractors: \citet{zou2026llm} standardise names and categories post hoc without checking extracted
interactions against the text that produced them. We are not aware of published work that verifies
literature-mined biotic-interaction triples against their source passage before ingestion.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

When candidates are retrieved by co-occurrence, the sentence-level question is answered before it
is asked, and a classifier built for it is left with little to decide. Telling the model which pair
the candidate is about --- with the same encoder and the same data --- raises AUPRC from $0.851$ to
$0.919$ on the biodiversity benchmark and from $0.738$ to $0.843$ on BioRED, and the relation term adds
nothing on top. The gain sits where the argument puts it: on passages that name more than one
candidate pair, and, where candidates come from automatic matching, on candidates whose arguments
are not organisms. The result is a calibrated score where a verdict stood, with a four-way direction
label beside it, cheap enough to run on every candidate.

\section*{Limitations}

\paragraph{One benchmark, singly annotated.}
437 rows in three blocks, every label assigned by one domain expert, with no independent second
grading: we report no inter-annotator agreement and cannot bound label noise. Some apparent false
positives are not model errors in the usual sense. A passage stating that ``the bat tick \ldots\ has
been reported \ldots\ to occasionally feed on humans'' is labelled negative, correctly, because the
curator judged the candidate's \emph{resolved} taxon, which retrieval had mapped to the wrong
species; the model sees only the surface form, which the passage does support. Errors of this kind
are upstream, and no passage-reading model can repair them. Candidates on which two independent
models confidently contradict the gold go to blind expert re-annotation, mixed with an equal number
of random controls and with labels and scores hidden; no label is changed on the strength of a
model's disagreement. On the recall side the denominator is 9 positives.

\paragraph{Top-ranked candidates only.}
Every \textsc{Biotx100} row is the top-ranked candidate for its passage, and half are pairs GloBI
already records. Whether the gains hold further down the retrieval ranking, where candidates are
noisier, is not measured here; \textsc{Test299} and the public benchmark of \S\ref{sec:biored} are
not rank-restricted.

\paragraph{The rules are partly fitted by choice.}
Four of the eight rules were selected after reading this benchmark's false positives
(\S\ref{sec:rules}). The training-data criterion bounds how much that selection can have memorised
the benchmark, but it does not remove it, and their effects should be read as optimistic until
confirmed on candidates neither the rules nor their author have seen. They fire on no row of a
separate 100-candidate sample, so that sample can neither confirm nor contradict them.

\paragraph{Fresh candidates are harder.}
On that sample --- 100 retrieved candidates drawn after the benchmark, half of them from passages
containing several candidate pairs --- all three models score well below their benchmark figures,
though in the same order: AUPRC $0.665$ sentence-level, $0.785$ pair-conditioned, $0.765$
triple-conditioned. Its label provenance is not identical to the benchmark's, so we do not read the
drop as measured, but it is the clearest sign that multi-pair passages remain the hard case and that
the benchmark may understate their share of a live pool.

\paragraph{The diagnosis is triple-level; the evaluation is pair-level.}
Only \textsc{Biotx100} carries whole-triple gold. Evaluating at the pair level everywhere means a
candidate with the right pair and a misbound relation counts as correct, so the comparison cannot
credit the relation term with the cases it alone could catch; triple-level gold on the other two
blocks is what would test whether the triple model's advantage at strict operating points is real.

\paragraph{BioRED's labels are document-level.}
A BioRED candidate is positive when the abstract relates the two concepts anywhere, so a co-mention
in a sentence that does not state the relation counts as positive. Both models are trained and scored
on the same noisy labels, so the comparison is controlled, but the absolute figures understate what
either would reach against sentence-level gold.

\paragraph{Teacher noise and pretraining exposure.}
The training labels come from a prompted teacher wrong about one time in seven, and the student
inherits that boundary; training on model-generated labels can degrade encoders
\citep{lu2025feeding}. The passages are published MEDLINE text and the teacher is a pretrained
model, so we cannot exclude that it saw them, and its labels may be optimistic for genuinely new
literature.

\paragraph{Upstream failures are out of reach.}
Of the 50 candidates the retrieval pipeline discarded, 32 carry an incorrect interaction term and 17
a mis-resolved taxon, 14 of them both. Those fail before any verifier is consulted, and no change to
the decision stage, ours included, addresses them.

\paragraph{One system, one domain.}
One retrieval system, one interaction vocabulary, taxon--taxon interactions in MEDLINE. The mechanism
--- a pair-agnostic decision over passages that name several candidate pairs --- is not specific to
ours, but we have not shown that other pools fail the same way.

The eight rules of \S\ref{sec:rules} are applied to the candidate before the verifier decides,
and each targets an error mechanism of \S\ref{sec:errors}. \emph{same\_organism}
(apposition of a common and a scientific name, or identical forms), \emph{own\_clade} (an adjacent
clade annotation such as ``(Acari: Prostigmata)''), and \emph{authority} (a taxonomic author parsed
as a taxon) detect the same-organism and entity classes. \emph{non\_biotic\_relation} rejects
phylogenetic, comparative and biogeographic relation terms; \emph{negated} rejects the two
negation forms whose scope provably contains one of the pair (``non-$s$ hosts'', ``no infections
with \ldots\ $s$''); \emph{not\_an\_organism} rejects organs and syndromes; \emph{pathogen\_modifier}
rejects an adjective that only ever occurs inside a pathogen's name; and \emph{co\_listed} rejects
two taxa that are conjuncts of one dependency-parsed list not governed by an interaction noun
(``interactions between $A$ and $B$'' is kept).

\paragraph{How they were validated, and what that licenses.}
No rule has a parameter fitted to evaluation labels, but rules can still be fitted by \emph{choice},
and four of these --- \emph{non\_biotic\_relation}, \emph{negated}, \emph{not\_an\_organism} and
\emph{pathogen\_modifier} --- were written after reading the false positives of \S\ref{sec:errors}. To keep their
selection from simply memorising the benchmark, every rule had to pass a criterion fixed before it
was evaluated: on the 42{,}747 training rows, at least 85\% of the candidates it rejects must be
teacher-negative, matching the teacher's own accuracy. Two first drafts failed it and were narrowed
on training data alone --- a general negation cue fired on ``without affecting the host'', and a
role-noun list rejected ``viruses'' and ``fungi'', which are legitimate coarse taxa --- and the
final rules reject teacher-negatives at $0.88$ to $1.00$ (\emph{co\_listed}, which needs a dependency
parse, on a fixed 6{,}000-row sample). The rules were then frozen, recorded by hash, and evaluated
once.

\section{Where the reformulation stops}
\label{sec:notfixed}

\paragraph{Entity disambiguation, and a gate that cannot fix it.}
The natural remedy for entity errors is to gate candidates on taxonomic validity. Against the Open
Tree Taxonomy \citep{rees2017opentree} such a gate catches 1 of the 35 unsupported \textsc{Biotx100}
candidates and discards 5 of the 65 supported ones. The reason is diagnostic: \emph{Illiger},
Dikarya, \emph{copia} and \emph{sulla} --- the surnames, clades, transposon families and vernacular
names behind the entity failures --- all \emph{resolve} in the taxonomy. The failure is
disambiguation, of the kind species-name normalisers address \citep{gerner2010linnaeus}, not
validity; and the gate's casualties are viruses, which are legitimate partners in this domain. The
\emph{authority} and \emph{same\_organism} rules (\S\ref{sec:rules}) target the disambiguation
directly and are the better instrument.

\paragraph{Direction needs its own gold.}
Expert curation of the benchmark marks reversed triples as correct --- we verified two by hand,
(\emph{Breinlia}, host of, \emph{Rattus}) and (\emph{Demidospermus}, parasitized by,
\emph{A.\ inermis}) --- so no directional number on the benchmark would measure the model rather
than the convention, which is why direction is scored on its own annotation (\S\ref{sec:deploy}). The
supervision itself is uneven in a revealing way: among the 5{,}591 reversed training rows, the rate
at which the teacher rejects a reversal is governed by the relation's \emph{surface string}.
Direction-encoding participles and prepositional forms are rejected at $0.51$--$0.70$
(\emph{affecting} $0.70$, \emph{affected by} $0.61$, \emph{parasite of} $0.57$), bare relational nouns
at $0.04$--$0.10$ (\emph{host of} $0.08$, \emph{pollinators} $0.04$). Whether supervision encodes
order at all depends on which string retrieval happened to match.

Table~\ref{tab:negative} collects interventions that did not pay, because each is a plausible first
thing to try. Self-distillation at equal capacity is null, suggesting the teacher's knowledge is
already saturated in the student. Compression to six layers buys throughput the pipeline does not
need for $0.088$ precision. Reweighting the corpus toward the deployment signal distribution cost

AUPRC unchanged within seed noise and the second cost $0.037$. Mechanisms that are properties of the
candidate are better answered by a rule than taught to a classifier. Deltas in the first four rows
were measured under an earlier version of the evaluation and are given as directions.
