\title{Pair-Conditioned Verification of Literature-Mined Biotic Interactions}

\author{Anonymous ACL submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Biotic interaction databases (who eats, infects or pollinates whom) are populated in part by text
mining. Candidates are proposed when a passage names two taxa near an interaction term, and the
obvious filter decides whether it describes an interaction. A passage naming several taxa may
support only some of its pairs, yet this filter gives them all the same label. In our benchmark,
passages naming three or more taxa hold 62\% of the interacting pairs. Keeping a 110M-parameter
encoder and its LLM-labelled training data fixed, we vary the input: the passage alone, with the
candidate pair, or with the pair and its interaction term. On 437 expert-graded
candidates, adding the pair raises the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) from 0.867 to
0.942 (the two names alone reach 0.808), and the interaction term adds no significant further gain. The gain is largest on passages
naming three or more taxa. It replicates on the public BioRED corpus and in zero-shot Qwen models
from 1.7B to 122B parameters, which rank candidates better when asked about the pair at every size.
Asked only about the passage, even the 32B model that labelled our data ranks below
the 110M encoder given the pair.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Databases such as GloBI \citep{poelen2014global} record which taxa eat, infect, parasitise or
pollinate which, and, like many biomedical databases \citep{hirschman2012biocuration,lee2018scaling},
they are populated in part by text mining \citep{dimitrova2020semantic}. A retrieval system proposes
candidate triples $(s_1, r, s_2)$, two organisms and an interaction term, with the passage
that allegedly supports them, and something must decide which candidates a curator should
review. A natural choice is a sentence-level classifier that reads the passage and decides whether it describes a biotic interaction.

The first two pairs are illustrative. Bottom: model inputs.}
\label{fig:concept}
\end{figure}

This classifier makes one decision per passage, whereas co-occurrence retrieval proposes candidates pair by pair: a
passage naming two taxa near an interaction term yields a candidate whether or not it asserts that
interaction between those two. Every candidate already contains the wording a sentence-level classifier responds to: in the retrieval pool we study
(\S\ref{sec:setting}), all 175{,}588 candidates carry an interaction term, and a lexical scan for
Relation Ontology interaction terms fires on 99.83\% of their passages. The decision that matters is narrower: does the passage assert an interaction between these two taxa? A passage that
names several organisms can support one pair and not another, and a classifier that sees only the
passage cannot tell which pair the candidate names: even a perfect one accepts the unsupported
pair (Figure~\ref{fig:concept}).

\citet{ruch2024biotxplorer}, who built the retrieval system, graded 100
random BiotXplorer triples, found that the system identified the interacting taxa with a
precision of 31\%, and named among the main causes of error ``instances where passages listed multiple
species, which can be automatically filtered out.'' On our benchmark (\bench{}, \S\ref{sec:benchmark}), passages naming three or more
taxa hold 156 of the 251 interacting pairs (62\%), so filtering them out would discard much of what
the pipeline exists to find. We keep those passages and judge each candidate by its pair.

To test this, we train the same 110M-parameter encoder three times on the same
corpus (labelled by a 32B language model), reading the passage alone (\emph{sentence-level}), the passage
with the candidate pair (\emph{pair-conditioned}), or the passage with the whole candidate triple
(\emph{triple-conditioned}), and compare them on 437 expert-graded candidates. Everything except the input is held fixed. Telling the model which pair it is judging raises the area under the
precision-recall curve (AUPRC, which summarises precision over all recall levels; a random ranking
scores the share of positives) from $0.867$ to $0.942$ and F1 from $0.790$ to $0.882$ (McNemar
$p = 3.8\times10^{-7}$). At equal review workload its F1 is higher at every share of candidates accepted between 4\% and 89\% (Figure~\ref{fig:curves}b), and adding the relation term
gives no significant further gain (\S\ref{sec:res-main}). On BioRED \citep{luo2022biored}, a public biomedical corpus, the same comparison raises
AUPRC from $0.738$ to $0.843$ and entity-pair F1 from $59.5$ to $69.6$, with the gain
concentrated on sentences that name more than two entities. Zero-shot Qwen models from 1.7B to 122B parameters show the same gap, and the verifier's recommended variant (\S\ref{sec:deploy}) runs
at 32 candidates per second on a CPU.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt\parskip0pt\topsep2pt
\item A controlled comparison of sentence-level, pair-conditioned and triple-conditioned
      verification on one encoder and one corpus, replicated on a public benchmark and in zero-shot
      Qwen language models from 1.7B to 122B parameters, with the gain concentrated on passages that name
      more than two entities, and a control given only the two names, which stays well below the
      pair-conditioned model (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\item An account of what giving the model the pair contributes, namely disambiguation between the organisms a passage
      names and a check on candidates whose arguments are not organisms, and a bound showing that
      checking a triple's components separately cannot reach the largest error class
      (\S\ref{sec:where}, \S\ref{sec:decomp}).
\end{itemize}
We also give operating curves for choosing a threshold; a practical configuration with
candidate rules, a four-way direction label, CPU throughput and escalation to a zero-shot large
language model (LLM) (\S\ref{sec:discussion}; Appendices~\ref{app:rules}--\ref{app:cascade}); and six negative results
(Appendix~\ref{sec:negative}).%

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Partial-input baselines.}
That a classifier can score well by exploiting how its data were assembled is well established in
NLP. Partial-input baselines recover much of the label from the hypothesis alone in natural language
inference
\citep{gururangan-etal-2018-annotation,poliak-etal-2018-hypothesis} and from the passage alone in
reading comprehension \citep{kaushik-lipton-2018-much}, and such shortcuts hold in distribution while
failing under deployment \citep{geirhos-etal-2020-shortcut,du-etal-2024-shortcut}. The closest
task-level precedent is fact verification \citep{thorne2018fever}, where a claim-only classifier
performs competitively with evidence-aware models because of how the corpus was built
\citep{schuster-etal-2019-towards}. The sentence-level filter of \S\ref{sec:intro} is also a
partial-input model. Here, though, the missing input is the candidate pair the pipeline itself proposed; in those
and other studies \citep{wiegand-etal-2019-detection,parmar-etal-2023-dont}, the shortcut comes
from how the data were collected. Partial-input results need careful
interpretation \citep{feng-etal-2019-misleading}, and our claims are limited to the models and corpora we study.

\paragraph{Entity pairs in relation extraction.}
Distant supervision assumes that a sentence containing two related entities expresses the relation
\citep{mintz2009distant}, and its standard relaxation exists because it does not
\citep{riedel2010modeling}. In supervised relation classification, as in TACRED \citep{zhang2017position}, an instance is the
triple (sentence, $e_1$, $e_2$) rather than the sentence, and how the pair is injected into the encoder
changes F1 by several points \citep{soares2019matching,wu2019enriching,zhou2022improved}.
\citet{rosenman2020exposing} already name our failure as an ``event heuristic'' that classifies on
(sentence, relation) while ignoring the arguments.  Our contribution is a controlled measurement of its effect in a verification setting built by co-occurrence
retrieval, replicated on BioRED and in zero-shot Qwen models,
and a bound on component-wise remedies. The entity-type shortcut usually invoked
for such errors \citep{peng2020learning,mtumbuka2024entity} is largely unavailable on \bench{}, where both slots
hold taxon surface forms, but the names alone predict part of the label (\S\ref{sec:res-ablation}). Error attribution across pipeline stages is
standard and easy to get wrong \citep{taille-etal-2020-lets,ai2023endtoend}.

\paragraph{Conditioning on the candidate.}
Casting a relation as a question about a named entity \citep{levy2017zero}, or as an entailment
hypothesis verbalising the relation between two of them \citep{obamuyide2018zero,sainz2021label},
is the reformulation our input format follows, and the resulting decision (does this passage
support this specific claim?)\ has the shape of scientific claim verification
\citep{wadden2020fact}, and of validating a knowledge-graph triple against text
\citep{lehmann2012defacto}, except that those systems retrieve evidence for a given claim or triple, while
here retrieval fixes the passage. Distilling a large model's judgements into a small student is
standard \citep{west2022symbolic,gekhman2023trueteacher}. Selective classification abstains when a confidence score falls below a
threshold \citep{geifman2017selective}, which is how extraction is made useful
to human abstractors \citep{swaminathan2024selective}.

\paragraph{Text mining for interaction databases.}
Judging an extracted fact before it reaches a curator is an old idea
\citep{rodriguezesteban2006imitating}, as is evaluating such tools by whether they help the curator
\citep{hirschman2012biocuration,britan2018nexta5,lee2018scaling}. \citet{cuzick2023interspecies},
independently, take the interacting pair rather than the passage as the annotation unit, the unit
our pair-binding error class (\S\ref{sec:errors}) isolates. Gold corpora for biodiversity text
annotate entities and the relations between them, such as microorganisms with their habitats and
phenotypes \citep{bossy2019bacteria} or organisms with environments, processes and qualities
\citep{abdelmageed2022biodivnere}. They serve to train and score extraction from text, whereas our
task is to verify candidates that retrieval has already proposed. \citet{farrell2024landscape} review text mining for ecology and evolution. Large language models mine ecological text
\citep{dsouza2025mining} and extract interactions at scale
\citep{keck2025extracting,zou2026llm}. \citet{zou2026llm}, working from citizen-science comments, standardise names and correct categories
by manual inspection, with no automatic step that checks each extraction against its text. We are not aware of published work
that verifies literature-mined biotic-interaction triples against their source passage before
ingestion.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Candidates and task definition}
\label{sec:setting}

BiotXplorer \citep{ruch2024biotxplorer} proposes candidate biotic-interaction triples from the
literature indexed by SIB Literature Services \citep{gobeill2020sibils}. For each passage it matches
taxon surface forms against a taxonomy and interaction surface forms against a vocabulary derived
from the Relation Ontology \citep{smith2005relations}, and emits candidate $(s_1, r, s_2)$ triples with the passage, the matched
surface strings and their canonical forms. We use it only as a source of candidates: no component of
it is a baseline in this paper. The pool we study has 175{,}588 candidate rows over 165{,}325
distinct passages and 115{,}404 distinct triple keys. Canonical strings often differ from what
the passage says, so we give the model the surface form. The
decision to make is whether the passage asserts an interaction between the candidate's two
organisms, in either direction (\S\ref{sec:protocol}).

\subsection{Training data and models}
\label{sec:verifier}

\paragraph{The teacher.}
Labels come from Qwen3-32B \citep{qwen2025technical}, a locally hosted open-weight model,
prompted zero-shot at temperature 0 (prompts in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}) and asked, per
candidate, whether the passage supports this triple. Its labels therefore vary across
candidates that share a passage. Against 150 expert-graded items its mislabel rate is
$22/150 = 14.7\%$ (Clopper--Pearson $[9.4\%, 21.4\%]$; 10 false positives, 12 false negatives).

\paragraph{The corpus.}
48{,}338 teacher-labelled rows, $0.343$ positive, drawn from the same retrieval system as the benchmark
(disjoint passages): a 20{,}000-row base sample, 16{,}546 \emph{contrast} rows, in which one passage
and its pair recur with different relation terms (on 2{,}017 passages with labels that disagree),
6{,}201 actively sampled rows, and 5{,}591 rows with the candidate's argument order reversed. The base
sample excludes the pairs of \textsc{Test299} and \textsc{Biotx100} (Appendix~\ref{app:paironly}).

\paragraph{Models and inputs.}
All three are cross-encoders on BiomedBERT-base (formerly PubMedBERT; 110M parameters; \citealp{gu2021domain}), distilled
from the teacher's labels \citep{bucilua2006model,hinton2015distilling} with the same recipe
(Appendix~\ref{app:artifacts}), keeping the checkpoint with the best AUPRC on a 10\%
development split grouped by taxon pair so that no pair (ignoring case and order) spans training and development. Three seeds
each; one run takes about four minutes on one A100. They differ only in their input.
The \textbf{sentence-level} model reads the passage alone. It is the simplest formulation, and on rows that share a passage it is shown the same input with different
labels, which it cannot fit. The \textbf{pair-conditioned} model reads the two candidate taxa,
$s_1$ \texttt{[SEP]} $s_2$, as segment~A and the passage as segment~B
\citep{nogueira2019passage}. The \textbf{triple-conditioned} model reads the whole candidate,
$s_1$ \texttt{[SEP]} $r$ \texttt{[SEP]} $s_2$. Comparing the three separates the pair's effect from the relation term's.

\subsection{An expert-graded benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}

Our benchmark, \bench{}, has 437 rows, 251 positive, in three blocks of different provenance. We report every
block as well as the pooled figures, since pooling alone hides their disagreement (\S\ref{sec:where},
Appendix~\ref{app:where}). \bench{} was built and graded by three of the authors, trained biomedical
curators. \textsc{Biotx100} consists of retrieved candidates, each with one passage chosen
by retrieval, expert-graded on four axes: whether each taxon surface form was correctly resolved,
whether the relation was correct, and whether the whole triple is supported by its passage. Half
were drawn from pairs already recorded in GloBI and half at random, and after removing three
training duplicates it contributes 97 rows: 50 GloBI-matched with 43 positive, 47 random with 33
positive.  \textsc{Reject50} consists of candidates an earlier filter rejected, expert-graded for the ones it should have kept: 41 clean rows, 11
positive. We use it to measure recall on candidates the earlier filter discarded (Appendix~\ref{sec:recall}). \textsc{Test299} is
299 expert-graded pair-level items, $0.548$ positive, whose passages most often name several
taxa.

The blocks do not carry the same gold: \textsc{Biotx100} and \textsc{Reject50} are also graded at the level of the whole
triple, while \textsc{Test299} was expert-graded at the pair level only and its stored relation term was
attached by retrieval. We therefore evaluate everywhere at the level all three share (``do these two taxa interact in this passage?''), and use the triple-level grades only to diagnose
(\S\ref{sec:decomp}). This can understate any benefit of triple conditioning, since a candidate with the
right pair and the wrong relation counts as correct.

One caveat applies: we scored roughly a hundred model configurations on this benchmark during development, so the recipes compared here were chosen with it in view. We expect the controlled comparison of \S\ref{sec:results} to be the least affected.

\subsection{Replication setting: BioRED}
\label{sec:biored-setup}

To test the claim on public data, we rebuild the same setting on BioRED \citep{luo2022biored}, a
corpus of PubMed abstracts with expert-annotated entities and relations. Candidates are generated
by co-occurrence: every pair of distinct annotated concepts co-mentioned in one sentence, restricted
to the eight entity-type pairs that carry relations in training, with the sentence as passage. A candidate is positive when BioRED relates
the two concepts anywhere in the abstract, a document-level label whose noise every model shares.
We follow the protocol of \citet{lai2025bioredirect}: models train on the 500 abstracts of BioRED's
training and development splits (15{,}640 candidates), select their epoch and threshold on its 100
test abstracts, and are scored on the 400 abstracts of the BioCreative VIII (BC8) test set
\citep{islamaj2024overview}. BioRED has no retrieved relation term, so only the sentence-level and
pair-conditioned formulations are compared, trained with the recipe of \S\ref{sec:verifier}.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

The threshold policy is fixed in advance. A model's reported operating point is either
\textbf{pre-specified}, chosen on its own training-development split and never on the benchmark, or
\textbf{block-held-out}: fitted on two of the three source blocks and applied to the third, so that
every row is scored by a threshold that never saw it and every row is used. Comparisons between
models use the same policy for both. Intervals are Clopper--Pearson for proportions and bootstrap for AUPRC. McNemar tests
\citep{mcnemar1947note} carry a continuity correction. With three seeds we report either the
per-seed mean and standard deviation or a three-checkpoint ensemble, which averages the checkpoints'
probabilities. The benchmark ships an \texttt{in\_train} flag on 9 rows. A 5-gram Jaccard scan
against all 48{,}338 training rows finds 3 more at Jaccard $1.000$, all in \textsc{Biotx100}. All 12 are
excluded throughout.

The gold is pair-level and therefore order-free, so a trained model that reads the candidate in order is
scored as the higher of its two scores over the two argument orders, a score for whether the
pair interacts in at least one direction. Otherwise a model would be penalised for rejecting a
correctly paired candidate whose stated relation runs backwards, which the gold does not count as an
error.

Table~\ref{tab:main} compares the three models on both benchmarks. On \bench{}, telling the model which pair a candidate is about raises
per-seed AUPRC from $0.867 \pm 0.006$ to $0.942 \pm 0.007$ and, at block-held-out thresholds, F1
from $0.790$ to $0.882$ (precision $0.752$ to $0.869$, recall $0.833$ to $0.896$). On decision
correctness the pair-conditioned model fixes 74 items and breaks 23, McNemar
$p = 3.8\times10^{-7}$. The advantage does not depend on calibration: compared at equal review
workload, the pair-conditioned model is ahead by $0.07$ to $0.09$ F1 whenever 50\% to 70\% of candidates are accepted, and the curves come within $0.01$ F1 of each other only when fewer than 10\% or more than 85\% of candidates are accepted (Figure~\ref{fig:curves}b; shares at six thresholds in Appendix~\ref{app:curve}).

Adding the relation term gives no significant further gain. The triple-conditioned model's AUPRC is level with the pair-conditioned model's ($0.945$ against $0.942$), and on decision correctness it fixes 21 items and breaks 30 ($p = 0.26$).

\subsection{Results on BioRED}
\label{sec:biored}

The result (Table~\ref{tab:main}, right; full table in Appendix~\ref{app:biored}) reproduces the
one on \bench{}. Conditioning on the pair raises AUPRC from $0.738$ to $0.843$ and F1 from $0.718$
to $0.817$, fixing 2{,}642 items and breaking 1{,}064. Marking the two mentions in the passage, the
standard relation extraction input \citep{zhou2022improved}, adds nothing to the pair input
($p = 0.080$).

On BioRED's standard document-level metric the pair-conditioned model reaches an entity-pair F1 of
$69.6$, against $59.5$ for the sentence-level one. Systems that read the whole abstract reach
$74.1$ to $75.3$ (PubMedBERT, \citealp{luo2022biored}; BioREx, \citealp{lai2023biorex}; BioREDirect; all as scored by \citealp{lai2025bioredirect})
and prompted GPT-4 $42.9$ \citep{islamaj2024overview}. Part of that gap is structural: over a fifth of
the related pairs are never co-mentioned in one sentence, so our sentence-scope candidates cap
recall at $0.776$. Given the whole abstract, the same model and recipe reach
$72.1$ (recall $0.757$, precision $0.690$), two to three points below the published systems. Those
also classify entity pairs, so the remaining gap reflects their document-level modelling and, for
BioREx, additional training corpora, problems separate from ours.

\label{sec:where}

Figure~\ref{fig:where} breaks the gain down by how many taxa the passage names, which is the most direct test of our explanation. On passages
that name three or more taxa, adding the pair raises AUPRC by $0.098$; on passages naming two or fewer taxa, by $0.043$. Splitting by argument shows where this smaller gain comes from: the candidates come from automatic matching, and 41\% of the 437 have at least one ``taxon'' that is a string
TaxoNERD does not recognise as an organism in the passage (an organ, an author's name, a vernacular
term). Where both arguments are recognised taxa and the passage names no third one, the case in which the sentence-level and pair-level decisions nearly coincide, the
pair input adds $+0.026$ on 33 items. Where an argument is not a taxon it adds $+0.049$, and $+0.180$
when the passage also names others. The pair input therefore does two things. It tells the model which pair to judge, and it lets the model check that both arguments are organisms. BioRED, whose entities are gold-annotated, isolates the first: on candidates from
sentences that name only two concepts the two models are indistinguishable ($0.909$ against
$0.902$), while on those from sentences naming more, which
are 97\% of the candidates, conditioning adds $+0.120$. By source block, the pair input raises
AUPRC from $0.835$ to $0.944$ on \textsc{Test299}, whose passages name the most taxa, from $0.964$
to $0.982$ on \textsc{Biotx100}, and from $0.551$ to $0.575$ on the 41-row \textsc{Reject50}, too
small to rank the models (Appendices~\ref{app:where} and~\ref{sec:recall}).

\subsection{Zero-shot language models}
\label{sec:zeroshot}

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig/fig_scaling.pdf}

\label{fig:scaling}
\end{figure}

The gap is not specific to small encoders: we put the two decisions of Figure~\ref{fig:concept} to zero-shot Qwen models as
yes/no questions, the sentence question and the pair question (Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}; scores are
$P(\textsc{yes})$, Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). Asked the
sentence question, the 32B teacher scores AUPRC $0.804$, below the 110M sentence-level
student; asked about the pair, $0.960$. Its gap, $+0.156$, is twice the student's (the two questions also differ in their instructions; Limitations), and it sits in
the same place: $+0.179$ on passages naming three or more taxa against $+0.107$ on the rest, where
the student's is $+0.098$ against $+0.043$. The 110M pair-conditioned student, about 300 times smaller than its teacher, scores $0.14$ AUPRC higher than the teacher asked the sentence question. As with the student, the
relation term adds nothing at this size ($0.964$ against $0.960$), although zero-shot models from
1.7B to 30B-A3B score higher on the triple question (Appendix~\ref{app:scaling}). From Qwen3-1.7B to Qwen3.5-122B-A10B \citep{qwen2026qwen35} the pair question leads at each size, by $+0.04$ to $+0.20$ AUPRC on
\bench{} and $+0.11$ to $+0.30$ on BioRED, while the sentence question gains little with size (Appendix~\ref{app:scaling}). Against its
teacher's labelling prompt ($0.964$) the pair-conditioned student gives up almost no F1 ($0.882$ against
$0.885$ at block-held-out thresholds) but $0.021$ AUPRC, about a quarter of which soft labels
recover (Appendix~\ref{app:soft}).

\subsection{Controls: input ablation, training data, encoder, names alone}
\label{sec:res-ablation}

Three controls locate the effect in the pair input. Degrading the trained triple-conditioned model's
segment~A at inference, weights and thresholds unchanged, shows which part of segment~A it uses: deleting both taxa
drops the three-checkpoint ensemble's AUPRC from $0.948$ to $0.691$ against a base rate of $0.574$, and emptying segment~A gives
$0.649$, while replacing or shuffling the relation, pair kept, moves it far less ($0.906$, $0.930$;
Appendix~\ref{app:ablation}). The triple-conditioned model does not need the corpus's contrast rows: trained
on the 20{,}000-row base sample alone, it reaches AUPRC $0.943 \pm 0.004$ (full corpus
$0.945 \pm 0.005$) and block-held-out F1 $0.886$. The encoder is not the cause either: of seven alternative recipes
screened on development data, the three that passed were scored once on test, none gains significantly on both
benchmarks, and with every encoder trained in both formulations the pair input wins, by $+0.103$ to $+0.113$ AUPRC on BioRED and
$+0.051$ to $+0.076$ on \bench{} (Appendix~\ref{app:encoders}).

A fourth control asks how much the two names predict on their own. A model trained with the same recipe on
the pair without the passage (\emph{pair-only}) reaches AUPRC $0.808$ on \bench{} and $0.710$ on
BioRED (Table~\ref{tab:main}), well above the base rates, and $0.134$ below the pair-conditioned
model on \bench{} (bootstrap 95\% interval $[0.085, 0.185]$). Only 38 of the 437 candidates have a
pair that occurs in the training corpus (ignoring case and order). On the other 399, adding the pair still raises
AUPRC from $0.870$ to $0.951$ (three-checkpoint ensembles), and on the 98 neither of whose taxa occurs
in training the sentence-level, pair-conditioned and pair-only models reach $0.920$, $0.980$ and $0.772$. Trained for up to ten epochs instead of
three, the pair-only model reaches $0.804$, so more epochs do not close the gap (Appendix~\ref{app:paironly}).

\subsection{Error analysis}
\label{sec:errors}
\label{sec:decomp}

The pair-conditioned model's 34 false positives at its block-held-out operating point are not
homogeneous: 24 come from passages naming three or more taxa, and 16 have an argument TaxoNERD does
not recognise as a taxon. Read by hand, they sort into a few mechanisms (examples in
Table~\ref{tab:examples}, Appendix~\ref{app:examples}; the per-row table will be released with the benchmark):
\emph{pair-binding}, where both taxa and the relation are correct but the relation's argument is a
third organism; \emph{co-listing}, where the two taxa are members of one enumeration and the passage
states no interaction between them; \emph{negation}, where the passage asserts the
opposite; \emph{same organism}, where the two ``taxa'' are one organism named twice or an organism
and its own clade; relation terms that name no biotic interaction
(\emph{migrating}); and \emph{entity} failures, where a
taxon string is a taxonomic author, a place, an organ, or an adjective inside a pathogen's name
(``\emph{avian} schistosomes'', which this model already rejects).

\paragraph{A bound on component-wise verification.}
The pair-binding class limits a natural alternative architecture. Its members have both taxa
correctly resolved and the correct relation. The error is a pairing made by retrieval that the passage does not state. A verifier
that checks $s_1$, $r$ and $s_2$ separately, however good each check is, cannot see it. On the 97
clean \textsc{Biotx100} candidates, where triple-level grades make the classes countable, 15 of the
32 unsupported candidates are pair-binding failures (the rest: 9 entity and 8 relation failures), a share of
$0.47$, Clopper--Pearson $[0.29, 0.65]$. A component-wise verifier therefore accepts these 15 at the same rate as the
65 supported triples, so its precision is at most
$65/(65+15) = 0.81$ at any recall, $[0.76, 0.87]$ across that interval. Only a model that reads the pair together with its passage can exceed this bound.

\subsection{Fresh candidates}
\label{sec:fresh}

We also scored 100 candidates from outside \bench{}: two further batches of 50 from the same
retrieval system, graded once at the pair level in the same expert curation as \textsc{Test299},
32 of them positive (a base rate of $0.32$, against $0.574$ on the benchmark). None of their
passages occurs in the benchmark or in the training corpus. As on the benchmark, segment~A uses the
taxon and relation strings as they appear in the passage. All three models score below their
benchmark figures, and the sentence-level model is again last: AUPRC $0.665$ sentence-level,
$0.787$ pair-conditioned and $0.751$ triple-conditioned (three-checkpoint ensembles), a lead of
$0.122$ for the pair-conditioned model (bootstrap 95\% interval $[0.010, 0.207]$). A lower base rate
lowers AUPRC by itself, but AUROC, which does not depend on it, drops too ($0.805$, $0.878$ and $0.843$,
against $0.842$, $0.932$ and $0.919$ on the benchmark), so the benchmark is likely easier than a live
pool and its absolute figures optimistic.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\paragraph{Partial input and model size.}
The remedy for a partial-input filter is to give it the missing input, here the candidate pair the pipeline proposed. Larger models do not make up for it. From 1.7B to 122B, zero-shot Qwen models answer the pair
question better than the sentence question at each size, and their answers to the latter gain
little with size. A 110M model given the pair ranks better than the 32B teacher asked about
the passage (\S\ref{sec:zeroshot}). The opposite partial input, the pair without its passage, also ranks below the
two together (\S\ref{sec:res-ablation}).

\paragraph{Passages naming only two taxa.}
The two decisions coincide when a passage names only the candidate's two entities and both are
what they claim to be: on BioRED sentences that name two concepts the two models are
indistinguishable, and on \bench{} the gain is smallest where both arguments are recognised
taxa and no third is named (\S\ref{sec:where}). Most of the positives in \bench{} come from passages that name
more.

\paragraph{Choosing an operating point.}\label{sec:opoint}
The precision of the pair-conditioned model ranges from $0.761$ at threshold $\tau = 0.01$ to $0.981$ at $\tau = 0.90$ (Appendix~\ref{app:curve}), so the right threshold depends on the use. Ingestion into a reference database calls for high precision, and assembling candidates for expert review for high recall. Reporting a score leaves this choice to the user \citep{geifman2017selective}.

\paragraph{A practical configuration.}\label{sec:deploy}
For use in a pipeline we recommend a variant of the pair-conditioned model that adds deterministic
candidate rules and a head labelling the interaction's direction in four categories
(Appendices~\ref{app:rules} and~\ref{app:deploy}). Its input and training labels also differ from
those of the pair-conditioned model, so it lies outside the controlled comparison. It scores 32
candidates per second on a CPU, and it can escalate the candidates it is least sure of to a
zero-shot LLM (Appendix~\ref{app:cascade}).

\paragraph{Errors made before verification.}
A wrong interaction term or a mis-resolved taxon arises during retrieval, before any verifier runs, and a model that reads the passage cannot see that the resolved taxon is not the one the passage names
(Limitations).

\paragraph{Other domains.}
The problem arises in any pipeline that proposes candidates by co-occurrence and filters them one
passage at a time. On BioRED the pair input also helps for genes, chemicals and diseases, though part
of that gain may come from entity types (Appendix~\ref{app:paironly}). For a new pipeline, the first
quantity to measure is the share of passages that name more than the candidate pair.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
When candidates come from co-occurrence retrieval, the model that verifies them should see the
candidate pair as well as the passage. A classifier that reads only the passage judges every pair in
it alike, and 62\% of the interacting pairs in \bench{} come from passages naming
three or more taxa. Giving the model the pair raises AUPRC from $0.867$ to $0.942$, with the largest
gain on those passages. The effect replicates on BioRED and in zero-shot Qwen models from 1.7B to
122B parameters. The verifier runs on a CPU, fast enough to score every candidate in the pool.

\section*{Limitations}

\paragraph{Benchmark size and annotation.}
\bench{} has 437 expert-graded rows in three blocks, each graded once. One of the authors, blind to the labels
and model scores, re-graded 22 candidates: 11 on which two models (triple-conditioned and
recommended, \S\ref{sec:deploy}) confidently disagreed with the gold, and 11 random controls. The
re-grade agreed with all 11 control labels (11/11, our only agreement figure) and changed 7 of the
other 11. One more label had been changed earlier, from positive to negative, after one of the
authors found that its \textsc{Test299} passage names \emph{Acanthamoeba} and \emph{Pseudomonas}
only as agents found together in the same horses. On the labels before the re-grade, which include
that change, the pair input raises AUPRC from $0.851$ to $0.918$. On the recall side (\textsc{Reject50}, Appendix~\ref{sec:recall}) the
denominator is 11 positives.

\paragraph{Rule selection.}
Every candidate rule was written with errors on this benchmark in view: four after reading its false
positives, \emph{co\_listed} after the other seven had been evaluated on it, and three from an earlier
filter's errors on two of its blocks (Appendix~\ref{app:rules}). The training-data criterion limits how
far the rules can be tailored to the benchmark but cannot exclude such tailoring, so the rules' effects should
be read as optimistic until they are confirmed on candidates that were not consulted when the rules
were written. On the fresh candidates of \S\ref{sec:fresh} the rules fire too rarely to confirm or
contradict these effects (Appendix~\ref{app:rules}).

\paragraph{Fresh candidates.}
On 100 fresh candidates from the same retrieval system (32 positive), all three models score
below their benchmark figures (\S\ref{sec:fresh}); part of the drop follows from the lower base rate,
but the absolute figures on \bench{} are likely to be optimistic.

\paragraph{Pair-level evaluation.}
\textsc{Test299}, the largest block, carries no whole-triple gold. Evaluating at the pair level
everywhere means a candidate with the right pair and the wrong relation counts as correct, so the
comparison cannot credit the relation term with the cases it alone could catch; measuring those would
need triple-level gold on \textsc{Test299}.

\paragraph{Document-level labels in BioRED.}
A candidate is positive when the abstract relates the two concepts anywhere, so a co-mention
in a sentence that does not state the relation counts as positive. Both models are trained and scored
on the same noisy labels, so the comparison is controlled, but the absolute figures need not carry
over to labels judged per sentence. BioRED candidates also share sentences, so candidate-level McNemar
tests and bootstrap intervals there are optimistic for small differences.

\paragraph{Prompt wording.}
Beyond naming the pair, the zero-shot pair question on \bench{} (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts})
asks the model to check that both strings name organisms in the passage and to reject a mere
co-mention or an interaction each has with a third organism, and it omits the sentence question's
examples of interactions (on BioRED its only added instruction is the co-mention clause), so part
of its lead in \S\ref{sec:zeroshot} may come from this wording rather than from naming the pair. The
trained models read no instructions and learn from the same labels; their inputs differ only by the
pair and, for the triple-conditioned model, the relation term, so the controlled comparison of
Table~\ref{tab:main} is unaffected.

\paragraph{Teacher noise and pretraining exposure.}
The training labels come from a prompted teacher wrong about one time in seven, and the student
inherits that boundary; training on model-generated labels can degrade encoders
\citep{lu2025feeding}. The passages are published PubMed text and the teacher is a pretrained
model, so we cannot exclude that it saw them, and its labels may be optimistic for genuinely new
literature. Asked about the pair without the passage, the teacher already ranks the \bench{}
candidates at AUPRC $0.871$ (Appendix~\ref{app:paironly}), so its labels may also reflect what it
knows about the two taxa.

\paragraph{Upstream failures.}
Most \textsc{Reject50} candidates carry an incorrect interaction term or a mis-resolved taxon
(Appendix~\ref{sec:recall}). Those fail before any verifier is consulted, and no change to
the decision stage, ours included, addresses them.

\paragraph{Single retrieval system and domain.}
We study one retrieval system with one interaction vocabulary, on interactions between taxa in PubMed titles and abstracts\@. The
mechanism, a pair-agnostic decision over passages that name several organisms, is not specific to
this pool, but beyond BioRED's gold-entity candidates (\S\ref{sec:biored-setup}) we have not shown
that pools from other retrieval systems fail the same way.

\section{Recall on rejected candidates}
\label{sec:recall}
\textsc{Reject50} asks whether candidates an earlier filter rejected contain recoverable
positives, and it is too small to rank the models: 11 positives, so one item moves recall by
nine points. At block-held-out thresholds the sentence-level model recovers 9 of the 11 by
re-admitting 13 of the 30 correct rejections (F1 $0.545$), the pair-conditioned model recovers 6
while re-admitting 5 ($0.545$), and the triple-conditioned model recovers 8 while re-admitting 3
($0.727$). We read only the qualitative point: the discarded candidates do contain recoverable
interactions, and a model that reads the candidate finds them with fewer wrong
re-admissions than one that reads only the passage. Most of these candidates fail before any
verifier runs: 27 of the 41 carry an incorrect interaction term and 12 a mis-resolved taxon, 10 of
them both.

\section{Entity disambiguation and direction}
\label{sec:notfixed}

\paragraph{Taxonomy gate.}
The natural remedy for entity errors is to gate candidates on taxonomic validity. Against the Open
Tree Taxonomy \citep{rees2017opentree} such a gate catches 1 of the 32 unsupported \textsc{Biotx100}
candidates and discards 5 of the 65 supported ones. This is because \emph{Illiger},
Dikarya, \emph{copia} and \emph{sulla}, the surnames, clades, transposon families and vernacular
names behind the entity failures, all resolve in the taxonomy. The failure is therefore one of disambiguation, of the kind species-name normalisers address \citep{gerner2010linnaeus}, which a validity gate cannot detect; and the gate's casualties are viruses, which are legitimate partners in this domain. The
\emph{authority} and \emph{same\_organism} rules (Appendix~\ref{app:rules}) target the disambiguation
directly and handle these cases better.

\paragraph{Direction labels.}
The benchmark's expert grading marks reversed triples as correct; we verified two by hand,
(\emph{Breinlia}, host of, \emph{Rattus}) and (\emph{Demidospermus}, parasitized by,
\emph{A.\ inermis}). No directional number on the benchmark would therefore measure the model rather
than the convention, which is why direction is scored on its own annotation
(Appendix~\ref{app:direction}). The supervision itself is also uneven: among the
5{,}591 reversed training rows, the rate at which the teacher rejects a reversal is governed by the
relation's surface string. For the relation strings with at least 40 reversed rows, the rejection
rate runs from $0.04$ to $0.70$ (\emph{affecting} $0.70$, \emph{affected by} $0.61$, \emph{pollinators}
$0.04$), and strings of the same form can differ widely (\emph{parasite of} $0.57$, \emph{host of}
$0.08$). Whether a training label encodes direction therefore depends on which string retrieval matched.

Several error mechanisms of \S\ref{sec:errors} concern the candidate rather than the
passage (whether two strings name one organism, whether a word is a surname, whether
\emph{relative to} is an interaction), and a classifier handles them poorly. We address them with
eight deterministic rejection rules, applied to the candidate before the verifier decides, each
targeting one of those mechanisms. The rules \emph{same\_organism}
(apposition of a common and a scientific name, or identical forms), \emph{own\_clade} (an adjacent
clade annotation such as ``(Acari: Prostigmata)''), and \emph{authority} (a taxonomic author parsed
as a taxon) detect the same-organism and entity classes. \emph{non\_biotic\_relation} rejects
phylogenetic, comparative and biogeographic relation terms; \emph{negated} rejects the two
negation forms whose scope provably contains one of the pair (``non-$s$ hosts'', ``no infections
with \ldots\ $s$''); \emph{not\_an\_organism} rejects organs and syndromes; \emph{pathogen\_modifier}
rejects an adjective that only ever occurs inside a pathogen's name; and \emph{co\_listed} rejects
two taxa that are conjuncts of one dependency-parsed list not governed by an interaction noun
(``interactions between $A$ and $B$'' is kept; parser: spaCy \texttt{en\_core\_web\_sm},
\citealp{honnibal2020spacy}).

Table~\ref{tab:rules} gives their effect on both models. On the pair-conditioned model they raise
precision by $0.009$ to $0.018$ and lower recall by $0.008$ to $0.020$, F1 unchanged ($p = 1.0$): of its 34
false positives they catch 6, because a model told which pair it is judging already rejects most of
what they target. On the triple-conditioned model they do more (precision $0.842 \to 0.883$,
$p = 0.17$). Both are upper estimates, since every rule was written with errors on this benchmark
in view (below); their main practical benefit is transparency, because each rejection names its
rule.

\paragraph{Validation.}
No rule has a parameter fitted to evaluation labels, but rules can still be fitted by choice.
Four of these (\emph{non\_biotic\_relation}, \emph{negated}, \emph{not\_an\_organism} and
\emph{pathogen\_modifier}) were written after reading the verifiers' false positives on this
benchmark (\S\ref{sec:errors}), \emph{co\_listed} was added after the other seven had been evaluated
on it, and \emph{same\_organism}, \emph{own\_clade} and \emph{authority} were written earlier, from an
earlier filter's errors on \textsc{Biotx100} and \textsc{Reject50}. To
keep their selection from simply memorising the benchmark, every rule had to pass a criterion fixed
before it was evaluated: on the 42{,}747 training rows in retrieved argument order, at least 85\% of
the candidates it rejects must be teacher-negative, matching the teacher's own accuracy. Two first drafts failed it and were
narrowed on training data alone (a general negation cue fired on ``without affecting the host'', and
a role-noun list rejected ``viruses'' and ``fungi'', which are legitimate coarse taxa), and, of the
candidates each final rule rejects, $0.88$ to $1.00$ are teacher-negative (\emph{co\_listed}, which
needs a dependency parse, on a fixed 6{,}000-row sample). The first seven rules were then frozen,
recorded by hash, and evaluated once; \emph{co\_listed} was written after that evaluation, then frozen
and evaluated in the same way. On the 100 fresh candidates of \S\ref{sec:fresh}, the four rules
written after reading the false positives reject one candidate (a negative) and \emph{co\_listed}
rejects six (five negatives), too few to confirm or contradict their effects.

The model we recommend for use (\S\ref{sec:deploy}) lies outside the controlled comparison. It is
one 110M-parameter model with two heads, and it differs from the pair-conditioned model of
\S\ref{sec:verifier} in three respects adopted during development: it gives the two taxa in
alphabetical order, so the input is identical whichever way round retrieval presents the pair; it
marks them in place in the passage (\texttt{@}\ldots\texttt{@}, \texttt{\#}\ldots\texttt{\#})
rather than only naming them in segment~A; and its training labels are the teacher's pair-level
judgements. Its interaction threshold, $0.5$, was set before evaluation and never tuned. On the 437
candidates it reaches AUPRC $0.970$ and F1 $0.915$ (precision $0.873$, recall $0.960$), and with the
candidate rules of Appendix~\ref{app:rules} it reaches precision $0.888$, recall $0.948$ and F1

true ones.} It scores $32.3$ candidates per second on eight CPU threads, measured with both heads.

precision with the rules from $0.888$ to $0.962$ at recall $0.912$ (Appendix~\ref{app:cascade}).

\paragraph{Direction head.}\label{app:direction}
The second head reports the interaction's direction in four categories. \textsc{Forward} and
\textsc{Reverse} say which taxon acts on the other; they come from a direction score that is exactly
antisymmetric in the two marked taxa, and are reported only when it is confident; otherwise the
label is \textsc{Uncertain}. \textsc{Bidirectional} is assigned when the relation is mutual by
definition (mutualism, symbiosis, competition, co-infection, \emph{interacts with}), as listed in a
relation-polarity lexicon derived from the Relation Ontology \citep{smith2005relations}; for those, neither taxon is the subject
and the model is not consulted. The benchmark's expert grading scores reversed triples as correct
(Appendix~\ref{sec:notfixed}), so direction is evaluated on 97 separately expert-graded candidates
(Table~\ref{tab:direction}): the model assigns a direction to 74\% of the directional ones, correctly on $0.887$ of
those, an optimistic figure since the abstention level was chosen on that set.

\section{Artefacts and compute}
\label{app:artifacts}

The training recipe of \S\ref{sec:verifier} is AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019decoupled} at learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$ and weight
decay $0.01$, a linear schedule with 10\% warm-up, batch size 32, inputs of at most 256 wordpieces (512 for the whole-abstract BioRED model of \S\ref{sec:biored}),
three epochs and gradient clipping at 1.0; Appendix~\ref{app:encoders} lists the alternatives we
screened. The encoders are BiomedBERT-base
(\texttt{microsoft/\allowbreak BiomedNLP-\allowbreak BiomedBERT-\allowbreak base-\allowbreak uncased-\allowbreak abstract-\allowbreak fulltext})
and -large (MIT licence) and BioLinkBERT-base and -large (Apache 2.0); the zero-shot models are Qwen3 0.6B--32B \citep{qwen2025technical} and Qwen3.5-122B-A10B \citep{qwen2026qwen35}, all Apache 2.0, run locally through
Ollama; taxon mentions are counted with TaxoNERD (MIT)\@. BioRED and its BioREDirect directionality
release are public NCBI resources, used as distributed. We will release our grades of \bench{}, the labels of the 100 fresh candidates and the teacher's
labels of the training corpus under CC BY 4.0, and the code under the MIT licence. The candidate
triples come from BiotXplorer (SIBiLS, CC BY 4.0). The passages are PubMed text (titles, abstracts and a
few keyword lists), courtesy of the U.S. National Library of Medicine; their copyright remains with
the publishers or authors, we distribute them with their PMIDs under NLM's terms, and a copy may
differ from the current PubMed record. The teacher labels were produced with Qwen3-32B (Apache
2.0). Eleven training rows are conflict-of-interest statements, some naming people; the release
leaves them out. Unless stated otherwise, everything ran on one
NVIDIA A100 (80\,GB): a three-epoch encoder run takes between one and twenty-five minutes (the two
eight-epoch runs of Appendix~\ref{sec:negative} longer), and the whole study took about 50 GPU-hours,
most of them LLM inference for labelling the corpus and for the zero-shot rows. The exceptions ran on CPUs: the three-epoch pair-only models of Appendix~\ref{app:paironly} (training and scoring), the zero-shot answers without the passage reported in that appendix, and the BioRED scores behind the entity-pair F1 of \S\ref{sec:biored}; Qwen3.5-122B-A10B ran partly on the CPU.

Table~\ref{tab:negative} collects interventions that did not help, because each is a plausible first
thing to try; the taxon-validity gate of its first row is described in Appendix~\ref{sec:notfixed}. Self-distillation at equal capacity is null, suggesting the teacher's knowledge is
already saturated in the student. Compressing to six layers speeds up CPU inference, which this setting does not need, and lowers precision by $0.088$. Reweighting the corpus towards the signal distribution of the retrieval
pool lowered AUPRC by $0.059$. The fifth row is the most informative. Before the candidate rules existed, we
turned the same mechanisms into training data (synthetic negatives for the same-organism,
own-clade and authority classes, and in a second variant for co-listing) and retrained. The first
variant left AUPRC unchanged within seed noise and the second lowered it by $0.037$. For properties of the candidate itself, rules worked better than extra training data. Deltas in
rows two to five were measured under an earlier version of the evaluation and are given as
directions. The last row is one of the three recipes of \S\ref{sec:res-ablation} that passed the
development screen (the others are in Appendix~\ref{app:encoders}): BiomedBERT-large raises the
BioRED pair-conditioned model's development AUPRC from $0.892$ to $0.905$ (three-seed means), but on
the BC8 test set the pair-conditioned model scores $0.843 \pm 0.005$ against $0.843 \pm 0.004$, F1
$0.814$ against $0.817$, EP F1 $69.7 \pm 0.5$ against $69.6 \pm 0.4$, and the sentence-level model
$0.741$ against $0.738$; the pair input's gain is unchanged ($+0.103$ against $+0.105$ AUPRC)\@. Beyond the development screen it was not trained on our
corpus or scored on \bench{}, since its BioRED test result could not change the choice.
For the whole-abstract BioRED model of \S\ref{sec:biored} we also tried, on development data only
(seed 1), eight epochs instead of three, alone and with each concept's BioRED type in segment~A:
development AUPRC $0.786$ and $0.791$ against $0.786$ for three epochs on the same seed, gains of at
most $0.005$, smaller than the $0.021$ spread of the three-epoch seeds ($0.765$--$0.786$), so neither was trained
further or scored on the test set.

The pair-only model of \S\ref{sec:res-ablation} is the pair-conditioned model of
\S\ref{sec:verifier} with the passage removed: it reads $s_1$ \texttt{[SEP]} $s_2$ as a single
segment. Encoder, corpus, seeds, recipe, checkpoint selection and threshold policy are those of the
pair-conditioned model on each benchmark; it is scored order-free like every trained model that reads the
candidate (\S\ref{sec:protocol}), and no model was scored more than once on a test set. Its per-seed
AUPRC is $0.807$, $0.798$ and $0.819$ on \bench{} and $0.714$, $0.697$ and $0.717$ on BioRED\@. On
\bench{} its development AUPRC was still rising at the third epoch, so we also trained it for up to
ten epochs, recipe otherwise unchanged, keeping the best development epoch (the fourth, third and
sixth for the three seeds). This longer-trained model reaches AUPRC $0.804 \pm 0.011$
(three-checkpoint ensemble $0.813$, against $0.812$ after three epochs), and the pair-conditioned
model still leads it by $0.133$ (bootstrap 95\% interval $[0.082, 0.186]$). All other pair-only
results are for the three-epoch model. The teacher's answers without the passage, below, give a
second names-only reference. On \bench{} the three-epoch model's block-held-out precision is $0.671$ at recall $0.952$, and its three-checkpoint
ensemble's AUPRC is $0.926$ on \textsc{Biotx100}, $0.800$ on \textsc{Test299} and $0.433$ on
\textsc{Reject50} (the other models' are in Table~\ref{tab:where}). Against the pair-only model, the
pair-conditioned model fixes 95 items and breaks 26 (McNemar $p = 6.3\times10^{-10}$), and the
sentence-level model fixes 80 and breaks 62 ($p = 0.15$). On BioRED its development thresholds are
low ($0.10$ to $0.16$), so its pre-specified operating point favours recall (precision $0.617$,
recall $0.859$); against it, the pair-conditioned model fixes 2{,}786 items and breaks 745
($p = 2.7\times10^{-258}$), and the sentence-level model, whose F1 is the same, fixes 2{,}906 and
breaks 2{,}443 ($p = 2.7\times10^{-10}$). Its entity-pair F1, $59.7 \pm 0.9$ (precision $0.532$,
recall $0.683$), is level with the sentence-level model's. On BioRED the two names also reveal the
candidate's entity types, a cue the sentence-level model cannot use because it is not told the pair,
so part of the pair-only model's score there, and of the pair-conditioned model's lead over the
sentence-level model, may come from the types.

\paragraph{Seen and unseen pairs.}
A \bench{} pair counts as seen when, lowercased and in either order, it is the pair of some row of
the 48{,}338-row training corpus, which holds 28{,}815 distinct pairs over 18{,}439 taxa; a taxon
counts as seen when it is an argument of some training row. An argument that lists alternative
names counts as seen if any of them matches (without this rule, 32 candidates have a seen pair instead of 38).
Pairs are counted against the whole corpus, development split included, so 3 to 6 of the 38
(depending on the seed) were seen only in development data. Matching is by surface string, as is
the pair grouping of the development split (\S\ref{sec:verifier}), so the same two taxa under other
names (a synonym, an abbreviated genus) count as unseen here and can fall on both sides of that
split; 38 is therefore a lower bound. Few benchmark pairs occur in training partly by design: the
corpus's 20{,}000-row base sample was drawn after removing pool rows whose pair matched, lowercased,
a pair of \textsc{Test299} or, through its canonical names, of \textsc{Biotx100}; \textsc{Reject50}
was not covered. On BioRED the training candidates of \S\ref{sec:biored-setup} define seen pairs,
by normalised surface string or by concept identifier. Table~\ref{tab:paironly} gives AUPRC on these
subsets.

The 38 \bench{} candidates with a seen pair are too few to separate the pair-only and
pair-conditioned models; on them, scoring each candidate by the share of its pair's training rows
labelled positive, with no model, already reaches $0.912$ ($0.598$ on all 437 rows, where unseen
pairs take the corpus base rate). On the 399 candidates with an unseen pair the pair-conditioned
model leads the sentence-level model by about as much as on all rows, and on the 98 candidates
neither of whose taxa occurs in training it reaches $0.980$, against $0.920$ for the sentence-level
model, while the pair-only model falls to $0.772$. On BioRED, too, the lead over the sentence-level
model holds on unseen pairs and the lead over the pair-only model widens where neither entity
occurs in training; its 381 candidates with a seen pair again do not separate the pair-only and
pair-conditioned models. Matching pairs by concept identifier instead (505 candidates with a seen
pair) leaves the pair-conditioned model's lead on the other 16{,}101 candidates at $+0.106$
$[0.096, 0.116]$ over the sentence-level model and $+0.128$ $[0.117, 0.139]$ over the pair-only
model.

\paragraph{The two halves of \textsc{Biotx100}.}
One half of \textsc{Biotx100} was drawn from pairs recorded in GloBI and the other at random
(\S\ref{sec:benchmark}). Of the GloBI half's 50 clean rows, 8 have a pair seen in training, against
14 of the 47 in the random half. On the GloBI half the pair-only model already reaches $0.964$ at a
base rate of $0.86$, so that half says little about reading the passage; the pair-conditioned model
leads the pair-only model by $0.030$ there and by $0.083$ on the random half, where the interval
includes zero (Table~\ref{tab:paironly}).

\paragraph{Zero-shot without the passage.}
The no-passage question, below, rewrites the pair question of Appendix~\ref{app:prompts} without the
passage: it asks whether both strings name organisms and whether the two are known to interact
directly, and it lists the sentence question's examples of interactions. Like the pair question, it
is asked in one argument order, and it differs from the pair question in wording as well as in input
(see the Limitations on prompt wording). Table~\ref{tab:nopassage} gives its AUPRC on \bench{} for
the Qwen3 models from 1.7B to 32B\@. At every size the pair question, which adds the passage, scores
$0.089$ to $0.134$ higher. Without the passage the 32B teacher scores $0.871$, above its answer to
the sentence question ($0.804$), so the names alone tell it much about which of these pairs
interact: it scores $0.991$ on the GloBI half of \textsc{Biotx100} and $0.833$ on the random half
($0.996$ and $0.951$ with the passage), and $0.883$ on the 399 candidates whose pair is unseen in training ($0.967$
with it). The models' answers without the passage improve with size as much as their answers to the
pair question ($0.690$ at 1.7B to $0.871$ at 32B, against $0.808$ to $0.960$), so the growth of the
pair question with size on \bench{} (Appendix~\ref{app:scaling}) need not come from reading the passage better.
