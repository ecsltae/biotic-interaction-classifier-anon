\title{Pair-Conditioned Verification of Literature-Mined Taxa Interactions}

\author{Anonymous ACL submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Taxa interaction databases (who eats, infects or pollinates whom) are partly built by mining the
literature. Candidates are proposed when a passage names two taxa near an interaction term, and the
obvious filter asks whether the passage describes an interaction. A passage naming several taxa may
support only some of its pairs, yet this filter gives them all the same answer. In our benchmark,
passages naming three or more taxa hold 62\% of the interacting pairs. Keeping a 110M-parameter
encoder and its LLM-labelled training data fixed, we vary the input: the passage alone, the passage
with the candidate pair, or the passage with the pair and its interaction term. On 437 expert-graded
candidates, adding the pair raises the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) from 0.867 to
0.942, and the interaction term adds no significant further gain. The gain is largest on passages
naming three or more taxa. It replicates on the public BioRED corpus and in zero-shot Qwen models
from 1.7B to 122B parameters, whose answers improve with size much more when asked about the pair.
Asked only about the passage, the 32B model that labelled our training data ranks candidates below
the 110M encoder given the pair.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Databases such as GloBI \citep{poelen2014global} record which taxa eat, infect, parasitise or
pollinate which, and like many biomedical databases \citep{hirschman2012biocuration,lee2018scaling}
they are populated in part by text mining \citep{dimitrova2020semantic}. A retrieval system proposes
candidate triples $(s_1, r, s_2)$, two organisms and an interaction term, together with the passage
that allegedly supports them, and something must decide which candidates a curator should
review. A natural choice is a sentence-level classifier that reads the passage and decides whether it describes a biotic interaction.

Bottom: model inputs.}

This classifier gives one answer per passage, whereas co-occurrence retrieval proposes candidates pair by pair: a
passage naming two taxa near an interaction term yields a candidate whether or not it asserts that
interaction between those two. Every candidate therefore already contains the wording a sentence-level classifier responds to: in the retrieval pool we study
(\S\ref{sec:setting}), all $175{,}588$ candidates carry an interaction term, and a lexical scan for
GloBI interaction vocabulary fires on $99.83\%$ of their passages. The decision that matters is narrower: does the passage assert an interaction between these two taxa? A passage that
names several organisms can support one pair and not another, and a classifier that sees only the
passage gives the same answer whichever pair the candidate names; even a perfect answer to its
question accepts the unsupported pair (Figure~\ref{fig:concept}).

\citet{ruch2024biotxplorer}, who built the retrieval system, graded 100
random BiotXplorer triples, found that the system identified the interacting taxa with a
precision of 31\%, and named the main cause of error: ``instances where passages listed multiple
species, which can be automatically filtered out.'' On our benchmark (\bench{}, \S\ref{sec:benchmark}), passages naming three or more
taxa hold 156 of the 251 interacting pairs (62\%), so filtering them out would discard most of what
the pipeline exists to find. We keep those passages and judge each candidate by its pair.

To test this, we train the same 110M-parameter encoder three times on the same
corpus, labelled by a 32B language model, reading the passage alone (\emph{sentence-level}), the passage
with the candidate pair (\emph{pair-conditioned}), or the passage with the whole candidate triple
(\emph{triple-conditioned}), and compare them on 437 expert-graded candidates. Everything except the input is held fixed. Telling the model which pair it is judging raises the area under the
precision-recall curve (AUPRC, which summarises precision over all recall levels; a random ranking
scores the share of positives) from $0.867$ to $0.942$ and F1 from $0.790$ to $0.882$ (McNemar
$p = 3.8\times10^{-7}$), and at equal review workload keeps F1 higher at every share of candidates accepted from 4\% to 89\% (Figure~\ref{fig:curves}b); adding the relation term
gives no significant further gain (\S\ref{sec:res-main}). On BioRED \citep{luo2022biored}, a public biomedical corpus, the same comparison raises
AUPRC from $0.738$ to $0.843$ and entity-pair F1 from $57.5$ to $67.1$, with the gain where the
argument predicts: absent on sentences that name only two entities, large on sentences that name
more. Zero-shot Qwen models from 1.7B to 122B parameters show the same gap, and the verifier runs
at 32 candidates per second on a CPU.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt\parskip0pt\topsep2pt
\item A controlled comparison of sentence-level, pair-conditioned and triple-conditioned
      verification on one encoder and one corpus, replicated on a public benchmark and in zero-shot
      Qwen language models from 1.7B to 122B parameters, with the gain located where the argument
      predicts (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\item An account of what the pair query contributes, namely disambiguation between the organisms a passage
      names and a check on candidates whose arguments are not organisms, and a bound showing that
      checking a triple's components separately cannot reach the largest error class
      (\S\ref{sec:where}, \S\ref{sec:decomp}).
\end{itemize}
We also give operating curves for choosing a threshold and a practical configuration with
candidate rules, a four-way direction label, CPU throughput and escalation to a zero-shot LLM
(\S\ref{sec:discussion}; Appendices~\ref{app:rules}--\ref{app:cascade}), and six negative results
(Appendix~\ref{sec:negative}).%

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Partial-input baselines.}
That a classifier can score well by exploiting how its data were assembled is well established in
NLP. Partial-input baselines recover much of the label from the hypothesis alone in natural language
inference
\citep{gururangan-etal-2018-annotation,poliak-etal-2018-hypothesis} and from the passage alone in
reading comprehension \citep{kaushik-lipton-2018-much}, and such rules hold in distribution while
failing under deployment \citep{geirhos-etal-2020-shortcut,du-etal-2024-shortcut}; the closest
task-level precedent is fact verification \citep{thorne2018fever}, where a claim-only classifier
matches evidence-aware models because of how the corpus was built
\citep{schuster-etal-2019-towards}. The sentence-level filter of \S\ref{sec:intro} is a
partial-input model in exactly this sense, except that the missing input is the query the pipeline
itself issued rather than an annotator artefact
\citep{wiegand-etal-2019-detection,parmar-etal-2023-dont}. As \citet{feng-etal-2019-misleading}
caution, our claims are limited to the models and corpora we study.

\paragraph{Entity pairs in relation extraction.}
Distant supervision assumes that a sentence containing two related entities expresses the relation
\citep{mintz2009distant}, and its standard relaxation exists because it does not
\citep{riedel2010modeling}. Since TACRED an instance has been the triple (sentence, $e_1$, $e_2$)
rather than the sentence \citep{zhang2017position}, and how the pair is injected into the encoder
changes F1 by several points \citep{soares2019matching,wu2019enriching,zhou2022improved}.
\citet{rosenman2020exposing} already name our failure, as an ``event heuristic'' that classifies on
(sentence, relation) while ignoring the arguments.  Our contribution is a controlled measurement of its effect in a verification setting built by co-occurrence
retrieval, replicated on BioRED and in zero-shot Qwen models and located where the argument predicts,
and a bound on component-wise remedies. The entity-type shortcut usually invoked
for such errors \citep{peng2020learning,mtumbuka2024entity} is unavailable here, since both slots
are filled from one inventory of taxon surface forms. Error attribution across pipeline stages is
standard and easy to get wrong \citep{taille-etal-2020-lets,ai2023endtoend}.

\paragraph{Conditioning on the query.}
Casting a relation as a question about a named entity \citep{levy2017zero}, or as an entailment
hypothesis verbalising the relation between two of them \citep{obamuyide2018zero,sainz2021label},
is the reformulation our input format instantiates, and the resulting decision (does this passage
support this specific claim?) has the shape of scientific claim verification
\citep{wadden2020fact}, and of validating a knowledge-graph triple against text
\citep{lehmann2012defacto}, except that those systems retrieve evidence for a given triple, while
here retrieval fixes the passage. Distilling a large model's judgments into a small student is
standard \citep{west2022symbolic,gekhman2023trueteacher}. Selective classification thresholds a score
instead of issuing a verdict \citep{geifman2017selective}, which is how extraction is made useful
to human abstractors \citep{swaminathan2024selective}.

\paragraph{Text mining for interaction databases.}
Judging an extracted fact before it reaches a curator is an old idea
\citep{rodriguezesteban2006imitating}, as is evaluating such tools by whether they help the curator
\citep{hirschman2012biocuration,britan2018nexta5,lee2018scaling}. \citet{cuzick2023interspecies},
independently, take the interacting pair rather than the passage as the annotation unit, the unit
our pair-binding error class (\S\ref{sec:errors}) isolates. Gold corpora for biodiversity text
annotate entities and the relations between them, such as microorganisms with their habitats and
phenotypes \citep{bossy2019bacteria} or organisms with environments, processes and qualities
\citep{abdelmageed2022biodivnere}; they serve to train and score extraction from text, whereas our
task is to verify candidates that retrieval has already proposed. Large language models mine ecological text
\citep{farrell2024landscape,dsouza2025mining} and extract interactions at scale
\citep{keck2025extracting,zou2026llm}; \citet{zou2026llm} standardise names and categories post
hoc, with no step that checks each extraction against its text. We are not aware of published work
that verifies literature-mined biotic-interaction triples against their source passage before
ingestion.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Candidates and task definition}
\label{sec:setting}

BiotXplorer \citep{ruch2024biotxplorer} proposes candidate biotic-interaction triples from the
literature indexed by SIB Literature Services \citep{gobeill2020sibils}. For each passage it matches
taxon surface forms against a taxonomy and interaction surface forms against a vocabulary derived
from the Relation Ontology, and emits candidate $(s_1, r, s_2)$ triples with the passage, the matched
surface strings and their canonical forms. We use it only as a source of candidates: no component of
it is a baseline in this paper. The pool we study has $175{,}588$ candidate rows over $165{,}325$
distinct passages and $115{,}404$ distinct triple keys. Canonical strings often differ from what
the passage says, so we query with the surface form, which is what the model can see. The
decision to make is whether the passage asserts an interaction between the candidate's two
organisms, in either direction (\S\ref{sec:protocol}).

\subsection{Training data and models}
\label{sec:verifier}

\paragraph{The teacher.}
Labels come from a locally hosted 32B open-weight instruction model \citep{qwen2025technical},
prompted zero-shot at temperature 0 (prompts in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}) and asked, per
candidate, whether the passage supports this triple. Its labels therefore vary across
candidates that share a passage. Against 150 expert-graded items its mislabel rate is
$22/150 = 14.7\%$ (Clopper--Pearson $[9.4\%, 21.4\%]$; 10 false positives, 12 false negatives).

\paragraph{The corpus.}
48{,}338 teacher-labelled rows, $0.343$ positive, drawn from the same retrieval pool as the benchmark
(disjoint passages): a 20{,}000-row base sample, 16{,}546 \emph{contrast} rows, in which one passage
and its pair recur with different relation terms (on 2{,}017 passages with labels that disagree),
6{,}201 actively sampled rows, and 5{,}591 rows with the candidate's argument order reversed.

\paragraph{Models and inputs.}
All three are cross-encoders on BiomedBERT-base \citep{gu2021domain} (110M parameters), distilled
from the teacher's labels \citep{bucilua2006model,hinton2015distilling} with the same recipe
(Appendix~\ref{app:artifacts}), keeping the checkpoint with the best development AUPRC on a 10\%
development split grouped by taxon pair so that no pair spans training and development. Three seeds
each; one run takes about four minutes on a single A100. They differ only in their input.
The \textbf{sentence-level} model reads the passage alone. It is the simplest formulation, and on rows that share a passage it is shown the same input with different
labels, which it cannot fit. The \textbf{pair-conditioned} model reads the two candidate taxa,
$s_1$ \texttt{[SEP]} $s_2$, as segment~A and the passage as segment~B
\citep{nogueira2019passage}. The \textbf{triple-conditioned} model reads the whole candidate,
$s_1$ \texttt{[SEP]} $r$ \texttt{[SEP]} $s_2$. Comparing the three separates the effect of the pair from that of the relation term.

\subsection{An expert-graded benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}

Our benchmark, \bench{}, has 437 rows, 251 positive, in three blocks of different provenance; we report every
block as well as the pooled figures, since pooling alone hides their disagreement (\S\ref{sec:where},
Appendix~\ref{app:where}). \textsc{Biotx100} consists of retrieved candidates, each with one passage chosen
by retrieval, expert-graded on four axes: whether each taxon surface form was correctly resolved,
whether the relation was correct, and whether the whole triple is supported by its passage. Half
were drawn from pairs already recorded in GloBI and half at random, and after removing three
training duplicates it contributes 97 rows: 50 GloBI-matched with 43 positive, 47 random with 33
positive.  \textsc{Reject50} consists of candidates an earlier filter rejected, expert-graded for the ones it should have kept: 41 clean rows, 11
positive. It is the paper's recall-side instrument (Appendix~\ref{sec:recall}). \textsc{Test299} is
299 expert-graded pair-level items, $0.548$ positive, and is where passages most often name several
taxa.

The blocks do not carry the same gold: \textsc{Biotx100} alone is graded at the level of the whole
triple, while \textsc{Test299} was expert-graded at the pair level and its stored relation term was
attached by retrieval. We therefore evaluate everywhere at the level all three share (``do these two taxa interact in this passage?''), and use the triple-level grades only to diagnose
(\S\ref{sec:decomp}). This can understate any benefit of triple conditioning, since a candidate with the
right pair and the wrong relation counts as correct.

One caveat applies: we scored roughly a hundred model configurations on this benchmark during development, so the recipes compared here were chosen with it in view; we expect the controlled comparison of \S\ref{sec:results} to be the least affected.

\subsection{Replication setting: BioRED}
\label{sec:biored-setup}

\bench{} is ours, built and graded by three of the authors. To test the claim
where anyone can check it, we rebuild the same setting on BioRED \citep{luo2022biored}, a public
corpus of PubMed abstracts with expert-annotated entities and relations. Candidates are generated
by co-occurrence: every pair of distinct annotated concepts co-mentioned in one sentence, restricted
to the eight entity-type pairs that carry relations in training, with the sentence as passage. A candidate is positive when BioRED relates
the two concepts anywhere in the abstract, a document-level label whose noise every model shares.
We follow the protocol of \citet{lai2025bioredirect}: models train on the 500 abstracts of BioRED's
training and development splits (15{,}640 candidates), select their epoch and threshold on its 100
test abstracts, and are scored on the 400 abstracts of the BioCreative VIII test set
\citep{islamaj2024overview}. BioRED has no retrieved relation term, so only the sentence-level and
pair-conditioned formulations are compared, trained with the recipe of \S\ref{sec:verifier}.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

The threshold policy is fixed in advance. A model's reported operating point is either
\textbf{pre-specified}, chosen on its own training-development split and never on the benchmark, or
\textbf{block-held-out}: fitted on two of the three source blocks and applied to the third, so that
every row is scored by a threshold that never saw it and every row is used. Comparisons between
models use the same policy for both. Intervals are Clopper--Pearson; McNemar tests
\citep{mcnemar1947note} carry a continuity correction. With three seeds we report either the
per-seed mean and standard deviation or a three-checkpoint ensemble, which averages the checkpoints'
probabilities. The benchmark ships an \texttt{in\_train} flag on 9 rows; a 5-gram Jaccard scan
against all 48{,}338 training rows finds 3 more at Jaccard $1.000$, all in \textsc{Biotx100}. All 12 are
excluded throughout.

The gold is pair-level and therefore order-free, so a model that reads the candidate in order is
scored as the higher of its two scores over the two argument orders, that is, the probability that
the pair interacts in at least one direction. Otherwise a model would be penalised for rejecting a
correctly paired candidate whose stated relation runs backwards, which the gold does not count as an
error.

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the comparison. On \bench{}, telling the model which pair a candidate is about raises
per-seed AUPRC from $0.867 \pm 0.006$ to $0.942 \pm 0.007$ and, at block-held-out thresholds, F1
from $0.790$ to $0.882$ (precision $0.752$ to $0.869$, recall $0.833$ to $0.896$). On decision
correctness the pair-conditioned model fixes 74 items and breaks 23, McNemar
$p = 3.8\times10^{-7}$. The advantage does not depend on calibration: compared at equal review
workload, the pair-conditioned model is ahead by $0.07$ to $0.09$ F1 whenever 50\% to 70\% of candidates are accepted, and the curves come within $0.01$ F1 of each other only when fewer than 10\% or more than 85\% of candidates are accepted (Figure~\ref{fig:curves}b; shares at six thresholds in Appendix~\ref{app:curve}).

Adding the relation term gives no significant further gain. The triple-conditioned model's AUPRC is level with the pair-conditioned model's ($0.945$ against $0.942$), and on decision correctness it fixes 21 items and breaks 30 ($p = 0.26$).

\subsection{Results on BioRED}
\label{sec:biored}

The result (Table~\ref{tab:main}, right; full table in Appendix~\ref{app:biored}) reproduces the
one on \bench{}. Conditioning on the pair raises AUPRC from $0.738$ to $0.843$ and F1 from $0.718$
to $0.817$, fixing 2{,}642 items and breaking 1{,}064. Marking the two mentions in the passage, the
standard relation extraction input \citep{zhou2022improved}, adds nothing to the pair query
($p = 0.08$).

On BioRED's standard document-level metric the pair-conditioned model reaches an entity-pair F1 of
$67.1$, against $57.5$ for the sentence-level one. Systems that read the whole abstract reach
$74.1$ to $75.3$ (PubMedBERT, BioREx and BioREDirect; \citealp{lai2023biorex,lai2025bioredirect})
and zero-shot GPT-4 $42.9$ \citep{islamaj2024overview}. Much of that gap is structural: a quarter of
the related pairs are never co-mentioned in one sentence, which caps any sentence-scope verifier at
recall $0.749$. Given the whole abstract, the same model and recipe reach $67.0$ (recall $0.697$,
precision $0.646$). The published systems also classify entity pairs, so the remaining gap comes from their document-level modelling, which is a separate problem from ours.

\subsection{Gain by number of taxa in the passage}
\label{sec:where}

Figure~\ref{fig:where} locates the gain; it is the most direct test of the argument. On passages
that name three or more taxa, adding the pair raises AUPRC by $0.098$; on passages naming two or fewer taxa, by $0.043$. Splitting by argument shows where this smaller gain comes from: the candidates come from automatic matching, and 41\% of the 437 have one ``taxon'' that is a string
TaxoNERD does not recognise as an organism in the passage (an organ, an author's name, a vernacular
term). Where both arguments are recognised taxa and the passage names no third one, the case in which the sentence question and the pair question nearly coincide, the
pair query adds $+0.026$ on 33 items; where an argument is not a taxon it adds $+0.049$, and $+0.180$
when the passage also names others. The pair query therefore does two things. It tells the model which pair to judge, and it lets the model check that both arguments are organisms. BioRED, whose entities are gold-annotated, isolates the first: on candidates from
sentences that name only two concepts the two models are indistinguishable ($0.909$ against $0.902$;
per seed $0.899$--$0.919$ against $0.892$--$0.914$), while on those from sentences naming more, which
are 97\% of the candidates, conditioning adds $+0.120$. By source block, the pair query raises
AUPRC from $0.835$ to $0.944$ on \textsc{Test299}, whose passages name the most taxa, from $0.964$
to $0.982$ on \textsc{Biotx100}, and from $0.551$ to $0.575$ on the 41-row \textsc{Reject50}, too
small to rank the models (Appendices~\ref{app:where} and~\ref{sec:recall}).

\subsection{Zero-shot language models}
\label{sec:zeroshot}

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig/fig_scaling.pdf}
\caption{Zero-shot Qwen models~\citep{qwen2025technical} asked the sentence question (dashed, open squares) or the pair question (solid, filled circles) on (a) the 437 expert-graded candidates of \bench{} and (b) a 3{,}000-candidate BioRED sample. Dotted: the trained 110M models on the same rows (three-checkpoint ensembles); grey: base rate. The x-axis spaces model sizes evenly. From 1.7B on, the pair question leads at every size, while answers to the sentence question change little with scale. $^*$Mixture of experts, placed by total size; the 32B model is the teacher. Qwen3.5-122B-A10B is a later release. Thin green line in (a): the triple question (\bench{} only), which matches or exceeds the pair question (Appendix~\ref{app:scaling}).}
\label{fig:scaling}
\end{figure}

The formulation effect is not a property of small encoders (Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}; scores are
$P(\textsc{yes})$, Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). Asked the
sentence question, the 32B teacher scores AUPRC $0.804$, below the 110M sentence-level
student; asked about the pair, $0.960$. Its gap, $+0.156$, is twice the student's (the two questions also differ in their instructions; Limitations), and it sits in
the same place: $+0.179$ on passages naming three or more taxa against $+0.107$ on the rest, where
the student's is $+0.098$ against $+0.043$. The 110M pair-conditioned student, about 300 times smaller than its teacher, scores $0.14$ AUPRC higher than the teacher asked the sentence question. As with the student, the
relation term adds nothing at this size ($0.964$ against $0.960$), although zero-shot models from
1.7B to 30B-A3B do gain from it (Appendix~\ref{app:scaling}). From Qwen3-1.7B to Qwen3.5-122B-A10B \citep{qwen2026qwen35} the pair question leads at each size, by $+0.04$ to $+0.20$ AUPRC on
\bench{} and $+0.11$ to $+0.30$ on BioRED, while the sentence question gains little with size. On
BioRED the teacher, asked the sentence question, accepts 92\% of its sample (AUPRC $0.566$ at a base
rate of $0.48$); asked about the pair, $0.813$, still behind the trained pair-conditioned model
($0.860$ on the same sample). Against its teacher's labelling prompt ($0.964$) the pair-conditioned student gives up $0.021$
AUPRC, of which distilling the teacher's probabilities recovers about a quarter
(Appendix~\ref{app:soft}). In F1 it gives up almost nothing at block-held-out thresholds ($0.882$ against the teacher's $0.885$; the soft-label student reaches $0.883$).

\subsection{Controls: query ablation, training data, encoder}
\label{sec:res-ablation}

Three controls locate the effect in the query. Degrading the trained triple-conditioned model's
query at inference, weights and thresholds unchanged, shows which part of the query it uses: deleting both taxa
drops the three-checkpoint ensemble's AUPRC from $0.948$ to $0.691$ against a base rate of $0.574$, and emptying the query gives
$0.649$, while replacing or shuffling the relation, pair kept, moves it far less ($0.906$, $0.930$;
Appendix~\ref{app:ablation}). The conditioned model does not need the corpus's contrast rows: a triple-conditioned
model trained on the 20{,}000-row base sample alone reaches AUPRC $0.943 \pm 0.004$ (full corpus
$0.945 \pm 0.005$) and block-held-out F1 $0.886$. The encoder is not the cause either: of seven alternative recipes
screened on development data, the three that passed were scored once on test, none gains significantly on both
benchmarks, and with every encoder trained in both formulations the pair query wins, by $+0.103$ to $+0.113$ AUPRC on BioRED and
$+0.051$ to $+0.076$ on \bench{} (Appendix~\ref{app:encoders}).

\subsection{Error analysis}
\label{sec:errors}
\label{sec:decomp}

The pair-conditioned model's 34 false positives at its block-held-out operating point are not
homogeneous: 24 come from passages naming three or more taxa, and 16 have an argument TaxoNERD does
not recognise as a taxon. Read by hand, they sort into a few mechanisms (examples in
Table~\ref{tab:examples}, Appendix~\ref{app:examples}; the per-row table is released):
\emph{pair-binding}, where both taxa and the relation are correct but the relation's argument is a
third organism; \emph{co-listing}, where the two taxa are members of one enumeration and the passage
states no interaction between them; \emph{negation}, where the passage asserts the
opposite; \emph{same organism}, where the two ``taxa'' are one organism named twice or an organism
and its own clade; candidates whose relation term names no biotic interaction at all
(\emph{relative to}, \emph{hybridization}, \emph{migration}); and \emph{entity} failures, where a
taxon string is a taxonomic author, a place, an organ, or an adjective inside a pathogen's name
(``\emph{avian} schistosomes'', which the pair-conditioned model already rejects).

\paragraph{A bound on component-wise verification.}
The pair-binding class limits a natural alternative architecture. Its members have both taxa
correctly resolved and the correct relation; the error is a pairing made by retrieval that the passage does not state. A verifier
that checks $s_1$, $r$ and $s_2$ separately, however good each check is, cannot see it. On the 97
clean \textsc{Biotx100} candidates, where triple-level grades make the classes countable, 15 of the
32 unsupported candidates are pair-binding failures (the rest: 9 entity, 8 predicate), a share of
$0.47$, Clopper--Pearson $[0.29, 0.65]$. A component-wise verifier accepting a fraction $r$ of the
65 supported triples therefore also accepts all 15, so its precision is at most $65r/(65r+15)$:
$0.81$ at full recall, $[0.76, 0.88]$ across that interval. Only a model that reads the pair together with its passage can exceed this bound.

\subsection{Fresh candidates}
\label{sec:fresh}

We also scored 100 candidates from outside \bench{}: two further batches of 50 from the same
retrieval system, graded once at the pair level in the same expert curation as \textsc{Test299},
32 of them positive (a base rate of $0.32$, against $0.574$ on the benchmark). None of their
passages occurs in the benchmark or in the training corpus. All three models score well below their
benchmark figures, and the sentence-level model is again last: AUPRC $0.665$ sentence-level,
$0.785$ pair-conditioned and $0.765$ triple-conditioned (three-checkpoint ensembles). Unlike the
benchmark's candidates, these were scored with the retrieval system's canonical names for the taxa
and the relation, which for most of them differ from the passage's wording (\S\ref{sec:setting}),
so the two conditioned models' figures are not directly comparable with their benchmark figures;
the sentence-level model reads no query and is unaffected. With 32 positives the size of the drop
is uncertain, but it suggests that the benchmark is easier than a live pool, so its absolute
figures are likely to be optimistic.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\paragraph{Partial input and model size.}
A sentence-level filter is a partial-input model in the sense of \S\ref{sec:related}, except that
the input it lacks is the query the pipeline issued, so the remedy is to put that query into
the input. Larger models do not make up for the missing input. From 1.7B to 122B, zero-shot Qwen models answer the pair
question better at each size while their answers to the sentence question gain little, and a
110M model asked about the pair ranks candidates better than the 32B teacher asked about
the passage (\S\ref{sec:zeroshot}).

\paragraph{Passages naming only two taxa.}
The two questions coincide when a passage names only the candidate's two entities and both are
what they claim to be: on BioRED sentences that name two concepts the two models are
indistinguishable, and on \bench{} the gain is smallest where both arguments are recognised
taxa and no third is named (\S\ref{sec:where}). Most of its positives come from passages that name
more.

\paragraph{Choosing an operating point.}\label{sec:opoint}
The precision of the pair-conditioned model ranges from $0.761$ at $\tau = 0.01$ to $0.981$ at $\tau = 0.90$ (Appendix~\ref{app:curve}), so the right threshold depends on the use. Ingestion into a reference database calls for high precision, and assembling candidates for expert review calls for high recall. Reporting a score leaves this choice to the user \citep{geifman2017selective}.

\paragraph{A practical configuration.}\label{sec:deploy}
For use in a pipeline we recommend a variant of the pair-conditioned model that adds deterministic
candidate rules and a head labelling the interaction's direction in four categories
(Appendices~\ref{app:rules} and~\ref{app:deploy}). Its input and training labels also differ from
those of the pair-conditioned model, so it lies outside the controlled comparison. It scores 32
candidates per second on a CPU, and it can escalate the candidates it is least sure of to a
zero-shot LLM (Appendix~\ref{app:cascade}).

\paragraph{Errors made before verification.}
Errors such as a wrong interaction term or a mis-resolved taxon arise during retrieval, before any verifier runs, and a model that reads the passage cannot see that the resolved taxon is not the one the passage names
(Limitations). Direction needs its own gold (Appendix~\ref{sec:notfixed}).

\paragraph{Other domains.}
Any pipeline that proposes candidates by co-occurrence and filters them passage by passage gives
every candidate from a passage the same answer; BioRED shows the remedy carrying over to genes, chemicals and diseases. For another pipeline, the first quantity to measure is the share of passages that name more than the candidate pair.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
When candidates come from co-occurrence retrieval, the model that verifies them should see the
candidate pair as well as the passage. A classifier that reads only the passage gives every pair in
it the same answer, and 62\% of the interacting pairs in \bench{} come from passages naming
three or more taxa. Giving the model the pair raises AUPRC from $0.867$ to $0.942$, with the largest
gain on those passages. The effect replicates on BioRED and in zero-shot Qwen models from 1.7B to
122B parameters. The verifier runs on a CPU, fast enough to score every candidate in the pool.

\section*{Limitations}

\paragraph{Benchmark size and annotation.}
\bench{} has 437 expert-graded rows in three blocks, each graded once. Labels were adjudicated
by a blind second annotation of 22 candidates: 11 on which two models (triple-conditioned and
recommended, \S\ref{sec:deploy}) confidently disagreed with the gold, and 11 random controls. It
kept every control label and changed 7; on the earlier labels the pair query raises AUPRC from
$0.851$ to $0.918$. On the recall side (\textsc{Reject50}, Appendix~\ref{sec:recall}) the
denominator is 11 positives.

\paragraph{Rule selection.}
Four of the eight candidate rules were selected after reading this benchmark's false positives
(Appendix~\ref{app:rules}). The training-data criterion bounds how much that selection can have memorised
the benchmark, but it does not remove it, and their effects should be read as optimistic until
confirmed on candidates neither the rules nor their author have seen. These four reject at most one
of the fresh candidates of \S\ref{sec:fresh}, too few to confirm or contradict them.

\paragraph{Fresh candidates.}
Scores drop on them (\S\ref{sec:fresh}).

\paragraph{Pair-level evaluation.}
Only \textsc{Biotx100} carries whole-triple gold. Evaluating at the pair level everywhere means a
candidate with the right pair and a misbound relation counts as correct, so the comparison cannot
credit the relation term with the cases it alone could catch; measuring those would need
triple-level gold on the other two blocks.

\paragraph{Document-level labels in BioRED.}
A candidate is positive when the abstract relates the two concepts anywhere, so a co-mention
in a sentence that does not state the relation counts as positive. Both models are trained and scored
on the same noisy labels, so the comparison is controlled, but the absolute figures understate what
either would reach against sentence-level gold.

\paragraph{Prompt wording.}
Beyond naming the pair, the zero-shot pair question on \bench{} (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts})
asks the model to check that both strings name organisms in the passage and to reject a mere
co-mention or an interaction each has with a third organism, and it omits the sentence question's
examples of interactions (on BioRED its only added instruction is the co-mention clause), so part
of its lead in \S\ref{sec:zeroshot} may come from this wording rather than from naming the pair. The
trained models read no instructions and learn from the same labels; their inputs differ only by the
pair and, for the triple-conditioned model, the relation term, so the controlled comparison of
Table~\ref{tab:main} is unaffected.

\paragraph{Teacher noise and pretraining exposure.}
The training labels come from a prompted teacher wrong about one time in seven, and the student
inherits that boundary; training on model-generated labels can degrade encoders
\citep{lu2025feeding}. The passages are published MEDLINE text and the teacher is a pretrained
model, so we cannot exclude that it saw them, and its labels may be optimistic for genuinely new
literature.

\paragraph{Upstream failures.}
Of the 41 \textsc{Reject50} candidates, 27 carry an incorrect interaction term and 12
a mis-resolved taxon, 10 of them both. Those fail before any verifier is consulted, and no change to
the decision stage, ours included, addresses them.

\paragraph{Single retrieval system and domain.}
We study one retrieval system with one interaction vocabulary, on interactions between taxa in MEDLINE. The
mechanism, a pair-agnostic decision over passages that name several organisms, is not specific to
this pool, but we have not shown that other pools fail the same way.

99.7\% of rows, and 24\% of the probabilities lie between $0.05$ and $0.95$) and trained the three
models again against these soft targets with a soft cross-entropy, selecting checkpoints on the
same hard-labelled development split. It is the only distillation variant we tried, scored once
(Table~\ref{tab:soft}). Every model gains a little AUPRC ($+0.005$ to $+0.007$), the
triple-conditioned model on every seed and with $0.021$ F1 ($p = 0.03$ before correcting for three
comparisons); against the pair-conditioned model it is marginally ahead ($0.951$ against
$0.947$; 25 items fixed, 21 broken, $p = 0.66$). The comparison is unchanged: the pair query lifts
AUPRC from $0.873$ to $0.947$ (72 fixed, 28 broken, $p = 1.7\times10^{-5}$), by $+0.095$ on passages
naming three or more taxa and $+0.036$ on the rest. Probabilities close about a quarter of the
pair-conditioned model's distance to its teacher's $0.964$ ($0.016$ AUPRC, against $0.021$ with
verdicts). The body keeps the verdict-trained models, on which every other analysis was run.

\section{Recall on rejected candidates}
\label{sec:recall}
\textsc{Reject50} asks whether candidates an earlier filter rejected contain recoverable
positives, and it is too small to rank the models: eleven positives, so one item moves recall by
nine points. At block-held-out thresholds the sentence-level model recovers 9 of the 11 by
re-admitting 13 of the 30 correct rejections (F1 $0.545$), the pair-conditioned model recovers 6
while re-admitting 5 ($0.545$), and the triple-conditioned model recovers 8 while re-admitting 3
($0.727$). We read only the qualitative point: the discarded candidates do contain recoverable
interactions, and a model that reads the candidate finds them with fewer wrong
re-admissions than one that reads only the passage.

\section{Entity disambiguation and direction}
\label{sec:notfixed}

\paragraph{Taxonomy gate.}
The natural remedy for entity errors is to gate candidates on taxonomic validity. Against the Open
Tree Taxonomy \citep{rees2017opentree} such a gate catches 1 of the 32 unsupported \textsc{Biotx100}
candidates and discards 5 of the 65 supported ones. This is because \emph{Illiger},
Dikarya, \emph{copia} and \emph{sulla}, the surnames, clades, transposon families and vernacular
names behind the entity failures, all resolve in the taxonomy. The failure is therefore one of disambiguation, of the kind species-name normalisers address \citep{gerner2010linnaeus}, which a validity gate cannot detect; and the gate's casualties are viruses, which are legitimate partners in this domain. The
\emph{authority} and \emph{same\_organism} rules (Appendix~\ref{app:rules}) target the disambiguation
directly and are the better instrument.

\paragraph{Direction labels.}
The benchmark's expert grading marks reversed triples as correct; we verified two by hand,
(\emph{Breinlia}, host of, \emph{Rattus}) and (\emph{Demidospermus}, parasitized by,
\emph{A.\ inermis}). No directional number on the benchmark would therefore measure the model rather
than the convention, which is why direction is scored on its own annotation
(Appendix~\ref{app:direction}). The supervision itself is also uneven: among the
5{,}591 reversed training rows, the rate at which the teacher rejects a reversal is governed by the
relation's surface string. Direction-encoding participles and prepositional forms are
rejected at $0.51$--$0.70$ (\emph{affecting} $0.70$, \emph{affected by} $0.61$, \emph{parasite of}
$0.57$), bare relational nouns at $0.04$--$0.10$ (\emph{host of} $0.08$, \emph{pollinators}
$0.04$). Whether a training label encodes direction therefore depends on which string retrieval matched.

Several error mechanisms of \S\ref{sec:errors} are questions about the candidate rather than the
passage (whether two strings name one organism, whether a word is a surname, whether
\emph{relative to} is an interaction), and a classifier handles them poorly. We answer them with
eight deterministic rejection rules, applied to the candidate before the verifier decides, each
targeting one of those mechanisms. \emph{same\_organism}
(apposition of a common and a scientific name, or identical forms), \emph{own\_clade} (an adjacent
clade annotation such as ``(Acari: Prostigmata)''), and \emph{authority} (a taxonomic author parsed
as a taxon) detect the same-organism and entity classes. \emph{non\_biotic\_relation} rejects
phylogenetic, comparative and biogeographic relation terms; \emph{negated} rejects the two
negation forms whose scope provably contains one of the pair (``non-$s$ hosts'', ``no infections
with \ldots\ $s$''); \emph{not\_an\_organism} rejects organs and syndromes; \emph{pathogen\_modifier}
rejects an adjective that only ever occurs inside a pathogen's name; and \emph{co\_listed} rejects
two taxa that are conjuncts of one dependency-parsed list not governed by an interaction noun
(``interactions between $A$ and $B$'' is kept).

Table~\ref{tab:rules} gives their effect on both models. On the pair-conditioned model they raise
precision by $0.009$ to $0.018$ and lower recall by $0.008$ to $0.020$, F1 unchanged ($p = 1.00$): of its 34
false positives they catch 6, because a model told which pair it is judging already rejects most of
what they target. On the triple-conditioned model they do more (precision $0.842 \to 0.883$,
$p = 0.17$). Both are upper estimates, since half the rules were chosen with this benchmark's errors
in view (below); their main practical benefit is transparency, because each rejection names its
rule.

\paragraph{Validation.}
No rule has a parameter fitted to evaluation labels, but rules can still be fitted by choice,
and four of these (\emph{non\_biotic\_relation}, \emph{negated}, \emph{not\_an\_organism} and
\emph{pathogen\_modifier}) were written after reading the false positives of \S\ref{sec:errors}. To
keep their selection from simply memorising the benchmark, every rule had to pass a criterion fixed
before it was evaluated: on the 42{,}747 training rows in retrieved argument order, at least 85\% of
the candidates it rejects must be teacher-negative, matching the teacher's own accuracy. Two first drafts failed it and were
narrowed on training data alone (a general negation cue fired on ``without affecting the host'', and
a role-noun list rejected ``viruses'' and ``fungi'', which are legitimate coarse taxa), and the
final rules reject teacher-negatives at $0.88$ to $1.00$ (\emph{co\_listed}, which needs a dependency
parse, on a fixed 6{,}000-row sample). The rules were then frozen, recorded by hash, and evaluated
once.

The model we recommend for use (\S\ref{sec:deploy}) lies outside the controlled comparison. It is
one 110M-parameter model with two heads, and it differs from the pair-conditioned model of
\S\ref{sec:verifier} in three respects adopted during development: it gives the two taxa in
alphabetical order, so the input is identical whichever way round retrieval presents the pair; it
marks them in place in the passage (\texttt{@}\ldots\texttt{@}, \texttt{\#}\ldots\texttt{\#})
rather than only naming them in segment~A; and its training labels are the teacher's pair-level
judgments. Its interaction threshold, $0.5$, was set before evaluation and never tuned. On the 437
candidates it reaches AUPRC $0.970$ and F1 $0.915$ (precision $0.873$, recall $0.960$), and with the
candidate rules of Appendix~\ref{app:rules} it reaches precision $0.888$, recall $0.948$ and F1
$0.917$ (5 items fixed, 3 broken, $p = 0.72$).\footnote{The \emph{co\_listed} rule is omitted from
this configuration: on this model's accept set it removes one false positive and three
true ones.} It scores $32.3$ candidates per second on eight CPU threads, measured with both heads.
Escalating the $30\%$ of candidates nearest its threshold to a zero-shot Qwen3.5-122B-A10B raises its
precision with the rules from $0.888$ to $0.962$ at recall $0.912$ (Appendix~\ref{app:cascade}).

\paragraph{Direction head.}\label{app:direction}
The second head reports the interaction's \emph{direction} in four categories. \textsc{Forward} and
\textsc{Reverse} say which taxon acts on the other; they come from a direction score that is exactly
antisymmetric in the two marked taxa, and are reported only when it is confident, otherwise the
answer is \textsc{Uncertain}. \textsc{Bidirectional} is assigned when the relation is mutual by
definition (mutualism, symbiosis, competition, co-infection, \emph{interacts with}), as listed in a
relation-polarity lexicon derived from the Relation Ontology; for those, neither taxon is the subject
and the model is not asked. The benchmark's expert grading scores reversed triples as correct
(Appendix~\ref{sec:notfixed}), so direction is evaluated on 97 separately expert-graded candidates
(Table~\ref{tab:direction}): the model answers 74\% of the directional ones, correctly on $0.887$ of
those, an optimistic figure since the abstention level was chosen on that set.

\section{Artifacts and compute}
\label{app:artifacts}

The training recipe of \S\ref{sec:verifier} is AdamW at learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$ and weight
decay $0.01$, a linear schedule with 10\% warm-up, batch size 32, inputs of at most 256 wordpieces,
three epochs and gradient clipping at 1.0; Appendix~\ref{app:encoders} lists the alternatives we
screened. The encoders are BiomedBERT-base
(\texttt{microsoft/\allowbreak BiomedNLP-\allowbreak BiomedBERT-\allowbreak base-\allowbreak uncased-\allowbreak abstract-\allowbreak fulltext})
and -large (MIT licence) and BioLinkBERT-base and -large (Apache 2.0); the zero-shot models are Qwen3 0.6B--32B and Qwen3.5-122B-A10B \citep{qwen2026qwen35} (Apache 2.0), run locally through
Ollama; taxon mentions are counted with TaxoNERD (MIT). BioRED and its BioREDirect directionality
release are public NCBI resources, used as distributed. Everything ran on one NVIDIA A100 (80\,GB):
an encoder run takes between two and fifteen minutes, and the whole study took about 50 GPU-hours,
most of them LLM inference for labelling the corpus and for the zero-shot rows.
